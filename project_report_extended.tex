\section{Abstract}
\label{sec:Abstract}
Stackelberg Security Games (SSGs) model the strategic allocation of limited
defender resources against attackers whose preferences are often uncertain.
\citet{balcan2015} designed no-regret online learning algorithms for the
\emph{repeated} variant of SSGs against an adversarial sequence of attackers
drawn from a finite, \emph{known} set $K$, and proved a matching linear-regret
lower bound when $K$ is unbounded. A natural real-world middle ground is
left open: the defender does not know $K$ a priori, but has a bound $\Kmax$
on its size. We first study this setting in the full-information
feedback model.
We propose \textsc{Grow-Catalogue}, an algorithm that incrementally learns
new attacker types as they arrive, re-partitions the coverage polytope, and
runs an anytime multiplicative-weights sub-routine on the resulting
extreme-point set. Our central technical contribution is a full proof that
\textsc{Grow-Catalogue} achieves expected regret
\[
O\!\left(n\Kmax\sqrt{T\log (n\Kmax)}\right)
\]
against any oblivious adversarial sequence of length $T$. This is the rate of
\citet{balcan2015} with $k$ replaced by $\Kmax$, inflated by the $\sqrt{\Kmax}$
that the unknown catalogue costs, and it is sublinear whenever
$n^2\Kmax^2\log(n\Kmax)=o(T)$. Experiments on synthetic SSG
instances confirm the predicted $\sqrt{T}$ scaling, and the empirical regret
stays comfortably below the analytic $C\sqrt{T}$ envelope.
We then ask whether the same relaxation is affordable when the defender
observes only the attacked target, and show that it is not: under
partial-information feedback with an unknown type set no algorithm achieves
sublinear regret, even when one fixed type attacks in every round, because
the defender can never learn \emph{which} type it faces. What is missing is
a name, so we identify the weakest supplement that supplies one, a
periodic report naming the set of types seen so far, and prove that it
restores regret
$O(n\Kmax^{4/3}T^{2/3}\log^{1/3}(n\Kmax)+\Kmax B)$ for a report period
$B$, the additive batching term being tight. Simulations of the lower-bound
constructions match the theory to machine precision.
Our implementation and all experiment scripts are at
\textcolor{blue}{\href{https://github.com/aditisingh1053/Security_Games}{github.com/aditisingh1053/Security\_Games}}.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In a Stackelberg Security Game (SSG), a defender allocates a scarce set of
resources to protect $n$ potential targets against a strategic attacker
who, observing the defender's randomized deployment, chooses the best
target to attack \citep{tambe2012}. Deployed systems built around this model
include the US Coast Guard's port protection patrols, the Federal Air
Marshals assignment system, the LAX airport patrol schedule, and
anti-poaching patrols in several African national parks
\citep{tambe2012,yang2014}. In every one of these applications the
defender must commit to its deployment before the attack happens, and the
attacker's payoff function encodes its preferences over targets.

The \emph{classical} SSG analysis assumes that the attacker's payoff
function is fully known to the defender. The optimal commitment can then
be computed by a linear program \citep[e.g.,][]{pita2010}. In practice,
however, the defender faces considerable uncertainty about the attacker.
There may be several distinct threat actors (e.g., different poacher
groups, different types of smugglers), and their utilities are only
imperfectly known. A principled response, pioneered by \citet{balcan2015},
is to drop the single-type assumption entirely and instead ask for a
\emph{no-regret} online algorithm against an adversarially chosen sequence
of attacker types, drawn from a known finite set $K$ with $|K|=k$. Under
full-information feedback, they design an algorithm whose expected
cumulative regret is bounded by $O(\sqrt{Tn^2 k\log(nk)})$, which is
sublinear in the horizon $T$, and they prove a matching linear-regret lower
bound when $|K|$ is unrestricted \citep[Theorem~7.1]{balcan2015}.

The assumption that the set $K$ itself is known to the defender in advance
is, however, a strong one. In realistic deployments the defender typically
does \emph{not} have a complete list of attacker types at the start. What
it may have is an \emph{upper bound} $\Kmax$ on the number of distinct
types it expects to encounter (for instance, from threat intelligence or
from physical constraints of the domain). The question that motivates this
project is therefore:
\begin{center}
\emph{Can the defender achieve sublinear regret when $K$ is unknown a
priori, subject to $|K|\le \Kmax$?}
\end{center}
This question lies squarely in the gap left open between the
\emph{known-$K$} upper bound and the \emph{unrestricted-$K$} lower bound of
\citet{balcan2015}. It is not answered by any of the existing work, and
resolving it is the main goal of the present report.

The answer turns out to depend sharply on what the defender is allowed to
observe, and this report is organized around that dependence.
\Cref{sec:model,sec:unknownK} set up the model and the unknown-$K$ problem,
and \Cref{sec:alg,sec:proof} give \textsc{Grow-Catalogue} together with its
guarantee under full-information feedback. \Cref{sec:partial} then withdraws
that feedback, leaving the defender only the attacked target, and shows that
the question now has a negative answer: no algorithm is sublinear, even
against a single fixed type. \Cref{sec:block} identifies the weakest
supplement that repairs this, a periodic report naming the types met so
far, and recovers a sublinear rate, with matching lower bounds on what
the batching costs. \Cref{sec:exp} reports the experiments and
\Cref{sec:conc2} the open problems.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contrib}

Our contributions are the following.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em,itemsep=0.10em,topsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Problem formalization (\Cref{sec:unknownK}).}
    We formalize the unknown-$K$ variant of the repeated Stackelberg
    security game, identifying the minimal modification of the model of
    \citet{balcan2015} that captures it.
  \item \textbf{Algorithm (\Cref{sec:alg}).}
    We propose \textsc{Grow-Catalogue}, an online algorithm that at each
    round maintains a \emph{catalogue} $C_t\subseteq K$ of attacker types
    observed so far, recomputes the set $\cE(C_t;\epsilon)$ of
    $\epsilon$-approximate extreme points of the partition of $\cP$
    induced by $C_t$, and runs an anytime multiplicative-weights
    sub-routine over $\cE(C_t)$. When a new type is observed, the
    catalogue is updated and the sub-routine is restarted on the enlarged
    expert set.
  \item \textbf{Main theorem and a complete, detailed proof
    (\Cref{sec:mainresult,sec:proof}).}
    The technical core of this report is a self-contained proof that
    \textsc{Grow-Catalogue} achieves expected regret at most
    $O(n\Kmax\sqrt{T\log (n\Kmax)})$ against any oblivious
    adversarial sequence of length $T$ under full-information feedback.
    The rate matches Theorem~5.1 of \citet{balcan2015} with $k$ replaced
    by $\Kmax$; for constant $\Kmax$ it is $\tilde O(n\sqrt T)$.
  \item \textbf{Regime analysis (\Cref{cor:regime,cor:adaptive}).}
    We characterize the parameter regime in which the bound is meaningful
    ($n^2\Kmax^2\log(n\Kmax)=o(T)$), and we prove an
    instance-adaptive refinement in which the leading factor depends on
    the number $K'$ of types \emph{actually} observed rather than on
    $\Kmax$.
  \item \textbf{An impossibility result under partial information
    (\Cref{sec:partial}).}
    We show that the two relaxations do not compose: if the defender
    observes only the attacked target and does not know $K$, then
    \Cref{thm:impossible} gives expected regret at least $T-2$, attained
    exactly, by \Cref{rem:tight-lb}, and this holds already at
    $\Kmax=1$, so the obstruction is not the number of types but the
    defender's inability to \emph{name} the one it faces. A separate
    phenomenon, \Cref{prop:sqrtT}, gives a $\Theta(\sqrt T)$ floor as soon
    as $\Kmax\ge 2$, on a universe of only two types and under full
    information.
  \item \textbf{Block revelation (\Cref{sec:block}).}
    We formalize in \Cref{prob:block} the weakest supplement that repairs
    the impossibility, every $B$ rounds an oracle reports the \emph{set}
    of types seen so far, and nothing about when or how often they attacked
   , give \Cref{alg:bgc} for it, and prove in \Cref{thm:block} a regret
    bound of $O(n\Kmax^{4/3}T^{2/3}\log^{1/3}(n\Kmax)+\Kmax B)$. By
    \Cref{cor:B} the report need arrive only $\tilde O(T^{1/3})$ times over
    the horizon, and not fewer.
  \item \textbf{Matching lower bound for the relaxation
    (\Cref{thm:blocklb}).}
    The additive $\Theta(\Kmax B)$ above cannot be removed: a nested
    threshold-interval construction inside $G_3$ forces regret at least
    $\Kmax(B-2)$ on every block-revelation algorithm, bracketing the
    batching cost as $\Theta(\Kmax B)$.
  \item \textbf{Experiments (\Cref{sec:exp}).}
    We implement \textsc{Grow-Catalogue} and two reference baselines and
    evaluate them on synthetic SSG instances; the empirical regret scales
    as predicted by the theorem, staying comfortably below the analytic
    $C\sqrt{T}$ envelope. An oracle reformulation (\Cref{app:oracle})
    removes the extreme-point enumeration and carries both algorithms to
    $n=10$ and $n=20$, and we simulate each lower-bound construction
    exactly, confirming that its constant is attained.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Classical SSGs and attacker uncertainty.}
The optimal commitment for a known attacker can be computed by an LP
\citep{conitzer2006}. Uncertainty about the attacker has been handled by
\emph{robust optimization}, which plans against worst-case payoffs
\citep{pita2010}, and by \emph{Bayesian SSGs}, which require a prior over
attacker types \citep{kiekintveld2011,marecki2012}. Both approaches rest on
priors or constraints that are often hard to validate. \citet{letchford2009}
give a query-efficient learning algorithm for a \emph{single} fixed but
unknown type, and \citet{blum2014b} extend this to a setting that adapts
round-by-round to one fixed type.

\paragraph{Online learning against adversarial attacker sequences.}
\citet{balcan2015} pivot from the single-type paradigm by allowing the
sequence of attackers to be adversarially chosen from a known finite set
$K$. For full information they give a polynomial-weights algorithm with
regret $O(\sqrt{Tn^2 k\log(nk)})$ that partitions the coverage polytope
into best-response regions and runs experts over the
$\epsilon$-approximate extreme points; for bandit feedback they use a
barycentric spanner construction to give an $O(T^{2/3})$ bound.
Crucially, they also prove a linear lower bound when $|K|$ is unrestricted,
which forces any non-trivial extension to restrict the attacker type set
somehow. We take the weakest restriction: a known cardinality bound
$\Kmax$.

\paragraph{Online learning with changing expert sets.}
The sub-routine inside \textsc{Grow-Catalogue} is an anytime variant of
Polynomial Weights / Hedge \citep{littlestone1994,freund1997,cesa2006}. We
restart this sub-routine each time the catalogue grows; simpler algorithms
for tracking a growing set of experts exist~\citep{mourtada2017}, and for
tracking a shrinking set \citep{gaillard2019}, but both would only shave
lower-order logarithmic factors.

\paragraph{Other recent directions.}
\citet{haghtalab2022} analyze no-regret learning against strategic
(non-myopic) attackers in a single-type query model. \citet{harris2024side}
extend to a contextual Stackelberg setting in which both players observe
side information. Neither addresses the \emph{unknown $K$ with bounded
cardinality} question that we pose here.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

We adopt the repeated-SSG notation of \citet[Sec.~2]{balcan2015}; our
notation is compatible with theirs.

\subsection{Game primitives}

The game has a finite horizon $T\in\NN$ and $n$ targets
$N=\{1,\dots,n\}$. The defender has $R$ resources and a collection
$\mathcal{D}\subseteq 2^N$ of \emph{schedules}, where each schedule is a
subset of targets that a single resource can defend simultaneously. An
\emph{assignment function} $A:R\to 2^{\mathcal{D}}$ records the schedules
that each resource may be assigned to. A \emph{pure strategy} is any valid
resource-to-schedule assignment; let $m$ denote the number of pure
strategies. A \emph{mixed strategy} is a distribution $\svec\in\Delta_m$
over pure strategies, and its induced \emph{coverage probability vector}
is $\pvec = M\svec\in [0,1]^n$, where $M\in\{0,1\}^{n\times m}$ has
$M_{ij}=1$ iff the $j$-th pure strategy covers target $i$. Write $\cP$ for
the set of valid coverage probability vectors;  Every coverage vector $\pvec$ is realizable by
some mixed strategy, and conversely every mixed strategy induces a unique
$\pvec\in\cP$.

\paragraph{Defender payoffs.}
For each target $i$, the defender has a payoff
$u_d^c(i)\in[-1,1]$ if $i$ is attacked \emph{and} covered, and a payoff
$u_d^u(i)\in[-1,1]$ if $i$ is attacked \emph{and uncovered}. The
defender's expected utility when target $i$ is attacked under coverage
$\pvec$ is therefore
\begin{equation}
U_d(i,\pvec)\;=\;u_d^c(i)\,p_i \;+\; u_d^u(i)\,(1-p_i),
\label{eq:Ud}
\end{equation}
which is \emph{linear} in $\pvec$ for each fixed target $i$.

\subsection{Attacker types and best response}

An attacker \emph{type} $\alpha$ is a pair
$(u_\alpha^c,u_\alpha^u)\in[-1,1]^n\times[-1,1]^n$ of per-target utilities
following the same ``covered/uncovered'' convention as the defender. Its
expected utility for attacking target $i$ under coverage $\pvec$ is
$
U_\alpha(i,\pvec)=u_\alpha^c(i)\,p_i + u_\alpha^u(i)\,(1-p_i).
$
Observing $\pvec$, type $\alpha$ \emph{best-responds}:
\begin{equation}
b_\alpha(\pvec)\;=\;\argmax_{i\in N}\,U_\alpha(i,\pvec),
\label{eq:br}
\end{equation}
with ties broken arbitrarily but consistently. Note that $b_\alpha$ is
piecewise constant in $\pvec$, which makes the defender's realized payoff
$U_d(b_\alpha(\pvec),\pvec)$ a discontinuous function of $\pvec$. This
non-continuity is the central technical difficulty that distinguishes SSGs
from vanilla online linear optimization, and it is what drives the
polytope-based machinery of \citet{balcan2015}.

\subsection{Repeated game and regret}

There is a (possibly unknown) set $K$ of attacker types. An
\emph{oblivious} adversary chooses a sequence
$\avec=a_1,\dots,a_T\in K^T$ \emph{before} the game starts; the sequence
is fixed but unknown to the defender. At each round
$t\in\{1,\dots,T\}$ the defender commits $\pvec_t\in\cP$, attacker
$a_t$ plays $b_{a_t}(\pvec_t)$, and the defender receives the random
payoff $U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)$. We assume \emph{full-information
feedback}: at the end of round $t$ the defender observes the identity of
$a_t$, and the first time any type $\alpha$ is observed, its utility
functions are revealed as well.

The defender's benchmark is the best fixed coverage vector in hindsight:
\begin{equation}
\pvec^{*}\;=\;\argmax_{\pvec\in\cP}\;\sum_{t=1}^T U_d\bigl(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec\bigr).
\label{eq:pstar}
\end{equation}
The expected regret of an online algorithm that plays $\pvec_t$ at round
$t$ is
\begin{equation}
\Regret(T)\;=\;\sum_{t=1}^T\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})\;-\;\EE\!\bigg[\sum_{t=1}^T\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)\bigg],
\label{eq:regret}
\end{equation}
where the expectation is over the algorithm's internal randomization.

\subsection{Preliminaries: polytope partition and $\cE(C;\epsilon)$}
\label{sec:polytopes}

The following definitions, taken from \citet[Sec.~4]{balcan2015}, are
essential. For any catalogue $C\subseteq K$, a type $\alpha\in C$, and a
target $i\in N$, the region in which $\alpha$'s best response is $i$ is
\begin{equation}
\cP^\alpha_i(C)\;=\;\{\pvec\in\cP\,:\,b_\alpha(\pvec)=i\},
\label{eq:Pia}
\end{equation}
which is the intersection of $\cP$ with the $n-1$ linear inequalities
$U_\alpha(i,\pvec)\ge U_\alpha(j,\pvec)$ for $j\ne i$; hence a convex
polytope. For a \emph{response profile}
$\sigma:C\to N$ we write
\begin{equation}
\cP_\sigma(C)\;=\;\bigcap_{\alpha\in C}\cP^\alpha_{\sigma(\alpha)}(C).
\label{eq:Psigma}
\end{equation}
The non-empty sets $\cP_\sigma(C)$ form a partition of $\cP$ into convex
regions in which the best response of every type in $C$ is held fixed;
within any such region, the defender's payoff under a fixed attacker type
is a linear function of $\pvec$.

We will need three results from \citet{balcan2015}, restated below in our
notation and cited explicitly at every use. \Cref{lem:43,lem:44} describe
the approximate extreme-point set; \Cref{prop:hedge} is the standard
Polynomial-Weights online learning guarantee, adapted to the present loss
range.

\begin{lemma}[{\citealp[Lemma 4.3]{balcan2015}}]
\label{lem:43}
For every $\epsilon>0$ and every catalogue $C\subseteq K$, there is a
finite set $\cE(C;\epsilon)\subseteq \cP$ of points, called the
$\epsilon$-approximate extreme points of the partition, such that for any
sequence $\avec'=a'_1,\dots,a'_{T'}$ with $a'_t\in C$ for all $t$,
\[
\max_{\pvec\in\cE(C;\epsilon)}\!\sum_{t=1}^{T'}\!U_d(b_{a'_t}(\pvec),\pvec)\,\ge\,\max_{\pvec\in\cP}\!\sum_{t=1}^{T'}\!U_d(b_{a'_t}(\pvec),\pvec)-2\epsilon\,T'.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[{\citealp[Lemma 4.4]{balcan2015}}]
\label{lem:44}
The set $\cE(C;\epsilon)$ of \Cref{lem:43} can be chosen so that
\(|\cE(C;\epsilon)|\in O\!\bigl((2^n+|C|\,n^2)^n\cdot n^{|C|}\bigr).\)
\end{lemma}

\begin{proposition}[{\citealp[Proposition 1]{balcan2015}}]
\label{prop:hedge}
For any fixed expert set $\cM$ and any sequence of losses in
$[-\kappa,\kappa]$, there is an online learning algorithm (an anytime
variant of Polynomial Weights / Hedge; see \citealp[Sec.~2]{cesa2006}) whose
expected regret after $T'$ rounds is at most
$O\bigl(\sqrt{T'\,\kappa\,\log|\cM|}\bigr)$, without requiring $T'$ to be
known in advance.
\end{proposition}

\section{The Unknown-$K$ Setting}
\label{sec:unknownK}

We now state the problem that our algorithm and theorem address.

\begin{problem}[Unknown-$K$ repeated SSG]\label{prob:unknownK}
A repeated SSG as in \Cref{sec:model} is played, with two modifications.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=0.05em,topsep=0.2em]
  \item The set $K$ of attacker types is not revealed to the defender at
  the start; however, the defender is given a constant $\Kmax\ge 1$ with
  the promise $|K|\le\Kmax$.
  \item Feedback is full information: after round $t$ the defender
  observes $a_t$, and the first time any type $\alpha$ appears, its
  utility vectors $(u_\alpha^c,u_\alpha^u)$ are revealed.
\end{enumerate}
The benchmark $\pvec^{*}$ and regret $\Regret(T)$ are as in
\Cref{eq:pstar,eq:regret}.
\end{problem}

\begin{remark}[Necessity of the bound $\Kmax$]\label{rem:necessity}
\citet[Theorem 7.1]{balcan2015} exhibit, for every $T$, a game with
$|K|<2^{T+1}$ on which every online algorithm has expected regret at
least $T/2$. Hence some finite bound on $|K|$ is \emph{necessary} for
sublinear regret; \Cref{prob:unknownK} takes the weakest such bound,
namely a known constant $\Kmax$.
\end{remark}

\section{Algorithm and Main Result}
\label{sec:alg}

\subsection{The \textsc{Grow-Catalogue} algorithm}

We describe \textsc{Grow-Catalogue} at a high level and then give the
pseudocode in \Cref{alg:gc}. At any round $t$ the defender holds a
catalogue $C_t\subseteq K$ of all types seen so far. Conceptually, the
defender runs a small instance of the \emph{known-$K$} algorithm of
\citet[Sec.~5]{balcan2015} on the catalogue $C_t$, pretending that $C_t$
\emph{is} the full attacker set. Concretely:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em,itemsep=0.05em,topsep=0.2em]
  \item The algorithm maintains the $\epsilon$-approximate extreme-point
  set $\cE_t=\cE(C_t;\epsilon)$ of \Cref{lem:43}. We set
  $\epsilon=1/\sqrt T$; this balances the $2\epsilon T$ approximation term
  in \Cref{lem:43} against the $\sqrt{T\log\Nmax}$ regret term of
  \Cref{prop:hedge}.
  \item An anytime Polynomial-Weights sub-routine of
  \Cref{prop:hedge} runs over $\cE_t$, updating via a time-varying
  learning rate $\eta_{t_{\text{loc}}}=\sqrt{\log|\cE_t|/t_{\text{loc}}}$
  where $t_{\text{loc}}$ is a local counter reset at every catalogue
  change. This is the standard anytime schedule; see \citet[Sec.~2]{cesa2006}.
  \item When a new attacker type $\alpha$ appears, the catalogue grows to
  $C_t\cup\{\alpha\}$, the approximate extreme-point set is recomputed,
  and the sub-routine is restarted on the enlarged expert set. The single
  round of first contact with $\alpha$ contributes a bounded additive
  amount to the regret that we will absorb.
\end{itemize}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Grow-Catalogue} (full information).}
\label{alg:gc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\small
\Require horizon $T$, bound $\Kmax$, game primitives, default $\pvec_{\text{def}}\in\cP$
\State $C\gets\emptyset$;~$\cE\gets\emptyset$;~$\epsilon\gets 1/\sqrt T$;~$t_{\text{loc}}\gets 0$
\For{$t=1,\dots,T$}
  \If{$\cE=\emptyset$} \State play $\pvec_t\gets\pvec_{\text{def}}$
  \Else \State sample $\pvec_t\sim q_t$, where $q_t$ is the current PW distribution over $\cE$
  \EndIf
  \State observe $a_t$
  \If{$a_t\notin C$} \Comment{discovery round: new attacker type}
    \State $C\gets C\cup\{a_t\}$; record $(u_{a_t}^c,u_{a_t}^u)$
    \State $\cE\gets\cE(C;\epsilon)$ \Comment{recompute extreme points}
    \State reset PW on $\cE$; $t_{\text{loc}}\gets 0$
  \Else \Comment{routine round}
    \State $t_{\text{loc}}\gets t_{\text{loc}}+1$
    \State compute $\ell_t(\pvec)\!=\!-U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)$ for $\pvec\in\cE$
    \State $\eta\gets\sqrt{\log|\cE|/t_{\text{loc}}}$
    \State $w_\pvec\gets w_\pvec\cdot e^{-\eta\,\ell_t(\pvec)}$ for all $\pvec\in\cE$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{Main Result}
\label{sec:mainresult}

\begin{theorem}[Main]
\label{thm:main}
Let the defender play \Cref{alg:gc} in the unknown-$K$ full-information
setting of \Cref{prob:unknownK}. For any oblivious adversarial sequence
$\avec\in K^T$ with $|K|\le\Kmax$, the expected regret satisfies
\begin{equation}
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;\le\;O\!\Bigl(n\Kmax\sqrt{T\log (n\Kmax)}\Bigr).
\label{eq:main}
\end{equation}
In particular, if $\Kmax=O(1)$ then $\EE[\Regret(T)]=\tilde O(n\sqrt T)$,
matching the known-$K$ bound of \citet[Theorem 5.1]{balcan2015} up to
logarithmic factors.
\end{theorem}

We prove \Cref{thm:main} in detail in \Cref{sec:proof}. Two useful
corollaries of the proof (the regime of applicability and an
instance-adaptive refinement) are stated after the proof in
\Cref{sec:consequences}.

% ==========================================================================
\section{Proof of \Cref{thm:main}}
\label{sec:proof}

The proof closely tracks the structure of the known-$K$ proof of \citet[Sec.~5]{balcan2015}
but has two non-trivial deviations: (i) the expert set is no longer static
but grows over time, and (ii) the discovery rounds introduce an extra
bounded additive term. We account for both the deviations in our proof.

\begin{proof}[Proof:- ]
Fix an oblivious adversarial sequence $\avec=a_1,\dots,a_T\in K^T$ with
$|K|\le\Kmax$. Let $K'=|\{a_1,\dots,a_T\}|\le\Kmax$ denote the number of
distinct types that actually appear in $\avec$. The proof proceeds in ten
steps.

\paragraph{Step 1 (Epoch decomposition).}
Let $\tau_1<\tau_2<\cdots<\tau_{K'}$ be the round indices at which each
new attacker type is first observed, i.e., for each
$i\in\{1,\dots,K'\}$, round $\tau_i$ is the smallest $t$ such that
$a_t\notin\{a_1,\dots,a_{t-1}\}$. We define the \emph{epochs}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Ep}_i\;=\;\{\tau_i+1,\tau_i+2,\dots,\tau_{i+1}-1\},\quad
i=1,\dots,K',
\label{eq:epoch-def}
\end{equation}
with the convention $\tau_{K'+1}\coloneqq T+1$, and write
$L_i=|\mathrm{Ep}_i|$. Let
$\mathcal{D}=\{\tau_1,\dots,\tau_{K'}\}$ be the set of \emph{discovery
rounds}. By construction,
\[
[1,T]\;=\;\mathcal{D}\;\sqcup\;\bigcup_{i=1}^{K'}\mathrm{Ep}_i,
\]
and
$|\mathcal{D}|+\sum_{i=1}^{K'}L_i=T$ with $|\mathcal{D}|=K'\le\Kmax$. The
epoch decomposition is the critical structural property of the proof: it
re-interprets the online problem with a growing expert set as a finite
sequence of online problems with \emph{fixed} expert sets, so that the
known-$K$ analysis of \citet{balcan2015} can be invoked inside each
epoch.

\paragraph{Step 2 (Catalogue is constant inside an epoch).}
Immediately after round $\tau_i$, the catalogue maintained by
\Cref{alg:gc} is $C_i=\{a_{\tau_1},\dots,a_{\tau_i}\}$, and it is not
updated until the next discovery round $\tau_{i+1}$. Hence $C_t=C_i$ for
every $t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i$. Moreover, by the defining property of
$\tau_{i+1}$, no new type appears in $\mathrm{Ep}_i$, so
\begin{equation}
a_t\in C_i\quad\text{for every }t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i.
\label{eq:epoch-types}
\end{equation}
Denote by $\cE_i\coloneqq\cE(C_i;\epsilon)$ the extreme-point set
used by the PW sub-routine during $\mathrm{Ep}_i$; \Cref{eq:epoch-types}
is exactly the hypothesis needed to apply \Cref{lem:43} to $C_i$ and the
sub-sequence inside $\mathrm{Ep}_i$.

\paragraph{Step 3 (Within-epoch regret).}
During $\mathrm{Ep}_i$ the algorithm runs a fresh instance of the anytime
Polynomial-Weights sub-routine on the fixed expert set $\cE_i$ for $L_i$
rounds. The losses are $\ell_t(\pvec)=-U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)\in[-1,1]$,
so the range parameter is $\kappa=1$. \Cref{prop:hedge} therefore
yields an absolute constant $c_1>0$ such that
\begin{align}
&\max_{\pvec\in\cE_i}\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)\notag\\
&\quad-\EE\!\left[\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)\right]\;\le\;c_1\sqrt{L_i\log|\cE_i|}.
\label{eq:hedge-epoch}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Step 4 (Within-epoch approximation via \Cref{lem:43}).}
We now wish to compare the max over $\cE_i$ during $\mathrm{Ep}_i$ with
the \emph{global} benchmark $\pvec^{*}$ (the best fixed strategy over the
full horizon $[1,T]$). \Cref{lem:43}, however, is a statement of the form
``$\max_{\pvec\in\cE(C;\epsilon)}(\cdot)\ge\max_{\pvec\in\cP}(\cdot)-2\epsilon T'$''
and, strictly speaking, does \emph{not} directly involve $\pvec^{*}$: it
compares the max over the finite set $\cE(C;\epsilon)$ against the
unrestricted max over $\cP$, both on the \emph{same} attacker sequence.
We therefore proceed in two sub-steps and make the passage to $\pvec^{*}$
explicit.

\emph{Sub-step 4a (apply \Cref{lem:43} to the epoch sub-sequence).}
By \Cref{eq:epoch-types}, the sub-sequence $(a_t)_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}$
has length $L_i$ and uses only types in $C_i$, which is exactly the
hypothesis of \Cref{lem:43}. Applying the lemma with catalogue $C_i$ and
sequence length $T'=L_i$ gives
\begin{align}
&\max_{\pvec\in\cE_i}\!\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)\notag\\
&\quad\ge\;\underbrace{\max_{\pvec\in\cP}\!\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)}_{\eqqcolon\;\mathrm{OPT}_i}-2\epsilon L_i.
\label{eq:lem43-epoch}
\end{align}
Here $\mathrm{OPT}_i$ is the best cumulative utility achievable on
$\mathrm{Ep}_i$ by \emph{any} coverage vector in $\cP$; crucially, this
is the epoch-$i$ optimum, which in general \emph{differs} from the value
of $\pvec^{*}$. This is the subtle point that distinguishes
our setting from the known-$K$ proof of \citet[Sec.~5]{balcan2015}: in their
analysis, \Cref{lem:43} is applied once on the full horizon, so
$\mathrm{OPT}=\sum_{t=1}^T U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})$ by
definition of $\pvec^{*}$ and no separate passage is needed. In our
per-epoch application, $\mathrm{OPT}_i$ is an epoch-local quantity,
potentially larger than $\pvec^{*}$'s value on the epoch.

\emph{Sub-step 4b (pass from $\mathrm{OPT}_i$ to $\pvec^{*}$).}
Since $\pvec^{*}\in\cP$, the coverage vector $\pvec^{*}$ is a \emph{feasible}
competitor in the maximization defining $\mathrm{OPT}_i$. Therefore
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{OPT}_i\;\ge\;\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*}).
\label{eq:opt-vs-pstar}
\end{equation}
Inequality \Cref{eq:opt-vs-pstar} is \emph{not} a consequence of
\Cref{lem:43} but of the trivial fact that the global benchmark
$\pvec^{*}$ is an element of the set over which the epoch-local optimum
is taken. Chaining \Cref{eq:lem43-epoch} with \Cref{eq:opt-vs-pstar},
\begin{align}
&\max_{\pvec\in\cE_i}\!\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)\notag\\
&\quad\ge\;\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})-2\epsilon L_i.
\label{eq:lem43-pstar}
\end{align}


\paragraph{Step 5 (Per-epoch combined bound).}
Chaining \Cref{eq:hedge-epoch,eq:lem43-pstar}, for every epoch
$i\in\{1,\dots,K'\}$ we obtain
\begin{align}
&\EE\!\left[\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)\right]\notag\\
&\;\ge\;\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})-2\epsilon L_i-c_1\sqrt{L_i\log|\cE_i|}.\label{eq:combined-epoch}
\end{align}
\Cref{eq:combined-epoch} is the per-epoch regret bound
\emph{against the global benchmark} $\pvec^{*}$, and it is the building
block for the rest of the proof. Note that despite the fact that
$\pvec^{*}$ is the benchmark over all $T$ rounds (not just epoch $i$),
we are able to bound our per-epoch regret against $\pvec^{*}$ because
$\pvec^{*}\in\cP$ and we applied \Cref{lem:43} on the sub-sequence.

\paragraph{Step 6 (Discovery rounds).}
For each discovery round $t\in\mathcal{D}$, the algorithm either plays
$\pvec_{\text{def}}$ (if $t=\tau_1$) or samples $\pvec_t$ from the PW
distribution inherited from $\mathrm{Ep}_{i-1}$ (if $t=\tau_i$ for
$i\ge 2$). In both cases the sampled $\pvec_t$ is not tuned to the
newly-appearing type $a_t=a_{\tau_i}$. However, because the payoff
$U_d$ is bounded in $[-1,1]$, for every $\pvec\in\cP$ and every type
$\alpha$, $U_d(b_\alpha(\pvec),\pvec)\in[-1,1]$, so
\begin{equation}
\bigl|U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})-U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)\bigr|\;\le\;2.
\label{eq:disc-bound}
\end{equation}
Summing \Cref{eq:disc-bound} over the at most $K'\le\Kmax$ discovery rounds
yields
\begin{equation}
\sum_{t\in\mathcal{D}}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})-\EE\!\left[\sum_{t\in\mathcal{D}}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)\right]\le 2\Kmax.
\label{eq:disc-sum}
\end{equation}
This is the only source of additive dependence on $\Kmax$ that does not
come from the main $\sqrt{T\Kmax}$ term. It is the ``price of not knowing
$K$'' at a discovery round.

\paragraph{Step 7 (Uniform bound on $|\cE_i|$).}
By \Cref{lem:44} applied with $|C_i|\le\Kmax$,
\[
|\cE_i|\;\le\;c_2\,(2^n+\Kmax n^2)^n\cdot n^{\Kmax}\;\eqqcolon\;\Nmax,
\]
for an absolute constant $c_2$. Taking logarithms,
\begin{align}
\log\Nmax &= n\log\!\bigl(2^n+\Kmax n^2\bigr)+\Kmax\log n+O(1)\notag\\
&\le n\bigl(n+1+\log(\Kmax n^2)\bigr)+\Kmax\log n+O(1)\notag\\
&= O\bigl(n^2+n\log(n\Kmax)+\Kmax\log n\bigr)\notag\\
&= O\bigl(n^2\,\Kmax\log(n\Kmax)\bigr).
\label{eq:logN}
\end{align}
Both summands of $2^n+\Kmax n^2$ are at least $1$, so their sum is at most
$2\cdot 2^n\cdot\Kmax n^2$, which gives the second line; the last step absorbs
each of the three terms into the product, every factor of which is at least
$1$ once $n\ge 2$. The third line is sharper and could be carried through; we
quote the fourth because it is the form \citet[Lemma 4.4]{balcan2015} take
into their Theorem 5.1, which lets \Cref{thm:main} be read off against that
theorem exactly. Crucially, \Cref{eq:logN} is uniform in
$i$: $\log|\cE_i|\le\log\Nmax$ for every epoch. This lets us pull the
$\log|\cE_i|$ factor in Step~5 outside the sum over epochs.

\paragraph{Step 8 (Summing across epochs).}
Sum \Cref{eq:combined-epoch} over $i=1,\dots,K'$ and add
\Cref{eq:disc-sum}. The algorithm's total expected utility is then at
least the benchmark's total utility, minus the discovery term $2\Kmax$,
minus $2\epsilon T$ from the telescoping Lemma-4.3 approximation errors
(using $\sum_i L_i\le T$), minus the sum of the per-epoch PW regret
terms. Rearranging,
\begin{align}
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;\le\;2\Kmax+2\epsilon T+c_1\sqrt{\log\Nmax}\!\sum_{i=1}^{K'}\!\sqrt{L_i}.
\label{eq:after-sum}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Step 9 (Cauchy--Schwarz).}
The last term involves the sum of $K'$ square roots. By the Cauchy--Schwarz
inequality applied to $(1,\dots,1)\in\RR^{K'}$ and
$(\sqrt{L_1},\dots,\sqrt{L_{K'}})$,
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^{K'}\!\sqrt{L_i}\;\le\;\sqrt{K'\cdot\!\sum_{i=1}^{K'}\!L_i}\;\le\;\sqrt{\Kmax\,T}.
\label{eq:CS}
\end{equation}
The first inequality is tight when all epochs have equal length, a
scenario that corresponds to an adversary who reveals types evenly across
the horizon. This is where the leading multiplicative $\sqrt{\Kmax}$
factor in the bound is generated. An instance-adaptive version uses $K'$
in place of $\Kmax$, which is what \Cref{cor:adaptive} records.

\paragraph{Step 10 (Final substitution).}
Plugging \Cref{eq:logN,eq:CS} into \Cref{eq:after-sum} and choosing
$\epsilon=1/\sqrt T$ gives
\begin{align*}
\EE[\Regret(T)]&\le 2\Kmax+2\sqrt T+c_1\sqrt{\Kmax T\,\log\Nmax}\\
&\le 2\Kmax+2\sqrt T\\
&\quad+c_3\,n\Kmax\sqrt{T\log (n\Kmax)}
\end{align*}
for an absolute constant $c_3$, the last line by substituting
\Cref{eq:logN} for $\log\Nmax$ and taking the square root of the product,
which collects the two factors of $\Kmax$ into one. Whenever $T\ge\Kmax$
(the only regime in which \Cref{thm:main} is non-trivial) the leading term
dominates the additive $2\Kmax+2\sqrt T$, so
\[
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;=\;O\!\Bigl(n\Kmax\sqrt{T\log(n\Kmax)}\Bigr),
\]
which is the bound of \Cref{thm:main}. Written in the equivalent form
$O(\sqrt{T\,n^2\Kmax^2\log(n\Kmax)})$, it is the bound of
\citet[Theorem 5.1]{balcan2015} with $k$ replaced by $\Kmax$ and inflated by
the $\sqrt{\Kmax}$ of the Cauchy--Schwarz step, so Step~9 is where the price
of the unknown catalogue is paid. This completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Consequences of the proof}
\label{sec:consequences}

Two corollaries follow directly from the proof of \Cref{thm:main} above.
The first characterizes the regime in which the bound is non-trivial,
the second gives an instance-adaptive refinement.

\begin{corollary}[Regime of applicability]
\label{cor:regime}
The bound of \Cref{thm:main} is sublinear in $T$ if and only if
$n^2\Kmax^2\log(n\Kmax)=o(T)$; equivalently,
$\Kmax=o\!\bigl(\sqrt T/(n\sqrt{\log (n\Kmax)})\bigr)$, which up to the
logarithmic factor is $\Kmax=\tilde o(\sqrt T/n)$.
In particular, for $\Kmax=O(1)$ the rate is $\tilde O(n\sqrt T)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Set the right-hand side of \Cref{eq:main} equal to $T$ and solve for
$\Kmax$. Squaring, $n\Kmax\sqrt{T\log(n\Kmax)}=o(T)$ holds exactly when
$n^2\Kmax^2\log(n\Kmax)=o(T)$, which is the stated condition; dividing by
$n^2\log(n\Kmax)$ and taking square roots gives the equivalent form.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Instance-adaptive bound]
\label{cor:adaptive}
Let $K'=|\{a_1,\dots,a_T\}|\le\Kmax$ be the number of \emph{distinct}
attacker types actually observed in the realized sequence. Then
\Cref{alg:gc} achieves
\[
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;\le\;O\!\Bigl(nK'\sqrt{T\log (nK')}\Bigr).
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$K'$ replaces $\Kmax$ at both places the proof uses it: the Cauchy--Schwarz
step \Cref{eq:CS} gives $\sum_{i=1}^{K'}\sqrt{L_i}\le\sqrt{K'T}$, and the
catalogue never holds more than the types observed, so $|C_i|\le K'$ and
Step~7 gives $\log\Nmax=O(n^2K'\log(nK'))$. Redoing Step~10 with both
gives the bound.\end{proof}

\section{Partial-Information Feedback with an Unknown Type Set}
\label{sec:partial}

\Cref{thm:main} shows that not knowing $K$ is affordable: under
full-information feedback it costs exactly a factor $\sqrt{\Kmax}$ over the
known-$K$ rate of \citet[Theorem 5.1]{balcan2015}, as Step~10 of
\Cref{sec:proof} records. Not observing $a_t$ is affordable
too: with $K$ known and only the attacked target observed,
\citet[Theorem 6.1]{balcan2015} achieve $O(T^{2/3}nk\log^{1/3}(nk))$. One
expects the two relaxations to compose. They do not.

We show that under partial information with an unknown type set no algorithm
achieves regret $o(T)$, \emph{even when $\Kmax=1$}, so that one fixed type
attacks in every round. The obstruction is not how many types the adversary
has, but that the defender can never learn \emph{which} type it faces: it
sees only a target index, and infinitely many types produce that same index.
The diagnosis is that the defender is missing a \emph{name}, and a name is a
bounded object, which is what \Cref{sec:block} then supplies.
\Cref{prop:sqrtT} closes the section by showing that this naming account is
special to $\Kmax=1$: two types already force $\Omega(\sqrt T)$ on a
two-element universe, under full information and with $K$ known.

\subsection{The problem}
\label{sec:partial-problem}

\begin{problem}[Unknown-$K$ repeated SSG, partial information]
\label{prob:partial}
A repeated SSG as in \Cref{sec:model} is played, with three modifications.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=0.05em,topsep=0.2em]
  \item There is a set $\Univ$ of attacker types, the \emph{universe}, known
  to the defender and not assumed finite.
  \item The set $K\subseteq\Univ$ is not revealed to the defender; it is
  given only a constant $\Kmax\ge 1$ with the promise $|K|\le\Kmax$.
  \item Feedback is \emph{partial information} \citep[Section 3]{balcan2015}:
  at the end of round $t$ the defender observes only the attacked target
  $b_{a_t}(\pvec_t)$. It never observes $a_t$, and utility vectors are never
  revealed.
\end{enumerate}
The adversary is oblivious, and $\pvec^{*}$ and $\Regret(T)$ are as in
\Cref{eq:pstar,eq:regret}.
\end{problem}

The defender's own payoff is not extra feedback: it knows $u_d^c$ and
$u_d^u$, so $U_d(i_t,\pvec_t)$ follows from the observed target and its own
play. And \Cref{prob:partial} is at least as hard as each setting it weakens:
revealing $a_t$ recovers \Cref{prob:unknownK}, and revealing $K$ at $t=0$
recovers \citet[Section 6]{balcan2015}.

\subsection{The gadget $G_3$}
\label{sec:gadget}

Every lower bound below is proved in one fixed game $G_3$: the game of
\citet[Theorem 7.1]{balcan2015}, with $n=3$ targets, one resource, and
schedules $\mathcal{D}=\{\emptyset,\{1\},\{2\},\{3\}\}$, so that
$\cP=\{\pvec\in[0,1]^3:p_1+p_2+p_3\le 1\}$. Letting the resource idle only
enlarges the defender's action set and so only strengthens the lower bounds;
every coverage vector we exhibit has $\sum_i p_i=1$, so each argument also
runs verbatim on the simplex. The defender's payoffs are
\begin{equation}
u_d^c(i)=u_d^u(i)=-1\ \ (i\in\{1,3\}),\qquad u_d^c(2)=u_d^u(2)=0,
\label{eq:g3-def}
\end{equation}
so that by \Cref{eq:Ud}
\begin{equation}
U_d(1,\pvec)=U_d(3,\pvec)=-1,\qquad U_d(2,\pvec)=0
\label{eq:g3-payoff}
\end{equation}
for every $\pvec\in\cP$. The defender's payoff therefore depends on $\pvec_t$
only through which target the attacker picks: a round is worth $0$ if
target~$2$ is attacked and $-1$ otherwise. We say the defender \emph{hits} at
round $t$ if $b_{a_t}(\pvec_t)=2$ and \emph{misses} otherwise. Types break
ties towards the lowest-indexed target, as in \citet[Section 7]{balcan2015};
at every benchmark we exhibit, target~$2$ is the \emph{strict} maximizer, so
the benchmark's value does not depend on the rule.

\paragraph{Threshold types.}
For $0\le r_1\le r_2\le 1$ let $\alpha_{r_1,r_2}$ be the attacker with
\begin{equation}
u^c_{\alpha}=(r_1-1,\;0,\;-r_2),\qquad
u^u_{\alpha}=(r_1,\;0,\;1-r_2),
\label{eq:type-utils}
\end{equation}
all six entries of which lie in $[-1,1]$. Substituting into
$U_\alpha(i,\pvec)=u^c_\alpha(i)p_i+u^u_\alpha(i)(1-p_i)$ gives, for every
$\pvec\in\cP$,
\begin{equation}
U_\alpha(1,\pvec)=r_1-p_1,\quad U_\alpha(2,\pvec)=0,\quad
U_\alpha(3,\pvec)=(1-r_2)-p_3 .
\label{eq:type-U}
\end{equation}
So on the segment $\pvec=(p,0,1-p)$ the three utilities are $r_1-p$, $0$ and
$p-r_2$, and comparing them gives
\begin{equation}
b_{\alpha_{r_1,r_2}}\bigl((p,0,1-p)\bigr)=
\begin{cases}
1 & p\in[0,r_1],\\
2 & p\in(r_1,r_2],\\
3 & p\in(r_2,1].
\end{cases}
\label{eq:type-line}
\end{equation}
This is \citet[Lemma 7.2]{balcan2015}; we record the utility vectors
explicitly because the arguments below need the best response at every
$\pvec\in\cP$, not only on that segment. A type is thus named by an interval
$(r_1,r_2]$, and the defender's task is to locate it.

The next lemma is the structural fact driving every lower bound: a hit is
very informative but very rare, because one coverage vector can hit at most
one type of any disjoint family.

\begin{lemma}[the hit condition]
\label{lem:hit}
Let $0\le r_1<r_2\le 1$ and $\alpha=\alpha_{r_1,r_2}$. For every $\pvec\in\cP$,
\begin{equation}
b_\alpha(\pvec)=2
\iff
p_1>r_1\ \text{ and }\ p_3\ge 1-r_2 ,
\label{eq:hit-iff}
\end{equation}
and then $p_1\in(r_1,r_2]$. Hence if the intervals of a family of types are
pairwise disjoint, every $\pvec\in\cP$ hits \emph{at most one} of them.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{eq:type-U} and lowest-index tie-breaking, target~$2$ is chosen
exactly when $U_\alpha(2,\pvec)=0$ strictly beats $U_\alpha(1,\pvec)=r_1-p_1$
and weakly beats $U_\alpha(3,\pvec)=(1-r_2)-p_3$, which is
\Cref{eq:hit-iff}. Then $p_1\le 1-p_2-p_3\le 1-p_3\le r_2$, so
$p_1\in(r_1,r_2]$; and a single $p_1$ cannot lie in two disjoint intervals.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Identification is a comparison search}
\label{sec:comparison}

Fix $M\ge 2$ and let
\begin{equation}
I_j=\Bigl(\tfrac{j-1}{M},\tfrac{j}{M}\Bigr],\qquad
\alpha^{(j)}=\alpha_{\frac{j-1}{M},\frac{j}{M}},\qquad j\in[M],
\label{eq:dyadic}
\end{equation}
so $I_1,\dots,I_M$ partition $(0,1]$ and
$\Univ_M\coloneqq\{\alpha^{(1)},\dots,\alpha^{(M)}\}$ consists of $M$
distinct types, to which \Cref{lem:hit} applies.

Facing an instance $K=\{\alpha^{(j)}\}$, the defender is searching for the
index $j$: each play is a query, each observation an answer in $\{1,2,3\}$,
and by \Cref{lem:hit} the answer ``$2$'' is consistent with at most one
candidate. The following lemma bounds how fast \emph{any} such search can
succeed. It sharpens the classical $\Omega(\log_2 M)$ comparison-search bound
in the two ways we need: it charges every unsuccessful query, not just the
number of queries until termination, and it bounds the whole tail of the
success time rather than only its worst case.

\begin{lemma}[comparison trees identify slowly]
\label{lem:count}
Let $M\ge 1$ and let $j$ be uniform on $[M]$. Consider any interactive
protocol in which, for $t=1,2,\dots$, a \emph{deterministic} strategy picks a
query $q_t$ as a function of everything seen so far and receives an answer
$o_t=\mathcal{O}(q_t,j)\in\{1,2,3\}$. Assume only that
\begin{equation}
\bigl|\{j'\in[M]:\mathcal{O}(q,j')=2\}\bigr|\le 1
\qquad\text{for every query } q .
\label{eq:one-hit}
\end{equation}
Let $\tau=\min\{t:o_t=2\}$, with $\tau=\infty$ if there is no such $t$. Then
for every $u\ge 1$ and every $T\ge 1$,
\begin{equation}
\Pr[\tau\le u]\;\le\;\frac{2^{u}-1}{M},
\label{eq:tau-tail}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\EE\bigl[\min\{\tau-1,\,T\}\bigr]\;\ge\;T-\frac{2^{T+1}-T-2}{M}.
\label{eq:count-final}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The idea is a counting one: a searcher that has not yet hit has learned only
which of $2^{t-1}$ answer histories it is in, and each such history can be the
prefix of a hit for at most one index.

Formally, let $H_t$ be the set of histories $(o_1,\dots,o_{t-1})$ of the first
$t-1$ rounds containing no answer $2$; each $o_s\in\{1,3\}$, so
$|H_t|\le 2^{t-1}$. If $\tau(j)=t$ then $j$ produces some $h\in H_t$; the
strategy being deterministic, the round-$t$ query is the fixed $q_t(h)$, and
$\mathcal{O}(q_t(h),j)=2$. By \Cref{eq:one-hit} at most one index answers $2$
to $q_t(h)$, so each $h$ accounts for at most one such $j$, giving
$|\{j:\tau(j)=t\}|\le 2^{t-1}$. Summing over $t\le u$ and dividing by $M$
gives \Cref{eq:tau-tail}.

For \Cref{eq:count-final}, put $X=\min\{\tau-1,T\}$. Since $X>s$ iff
$\tau>s+1$,
\begin{align*}
\EE[X]&=\sum_{s=0}^{T-1}\Pr[X>s]=\sum_{u=1}^{T}\bigl(1-\Pr[\tau\le u]\bigr)\\
&\ge\ T-\sum_{u=1}^{T}\frac{2^{u}-1}{M},
\end{align*}
which is \Cref{eq:count-final} because $\sum_{u\le T}2^u=2^{T+1}-2$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The impossibility theorem}
\label{sec:impossibility}

\begin{theorem}[unknown $K$ with partial information is not learnable]
\label{thm:impossible}
Let $G_3$ be as in \Cref{eq:g3-def} and let
$\Univ=\{\alpha_{r_1,r_2}:0\le r_1\le r_2\le 1\}$ be the infinite universe of
threshold types \Cref{eq:type-utils}. For every $T\ge 1$ and every
$\Kmax\ge 1$, every online algorithm for \Cref{prob:partial}, randomized
or deterministic, even one knowing $T$, $\Univ$, $\Kmax$ and the
construction, suffers, on some instance with $|K|=1$,
\begin{equation}
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;\ge\;T-2+\frac{T+2}{2^{T}}\;>\;T-2 .
\label{eq:impossible}
\end{equation}
The game does not depend on $T$; only the adversary's choice of $K$ inside
the fixed universe does.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Set $M=2^{T}$ and take $\Univ_M\subset\Univ$ as in \Cref{eq:dyadic}. By
Yao's minimax principle it suffices to exhibit a distribution over instances
against which every \emph{deterministic} algorithm suffers this much: draw
$j$ uniformly from $[M]$ and set $K=\{\alpha^{(j)}\}$, with
$a_t=\alpha^{(j)}$ for all $t$. This is legal for every $\Kmax\ge1$ and
oblivious, since $\avec$ is fixed before play.

\emph{The benchmark is $0$.} Let
$\pvec^{*}=\bigl(\tfrac{2j-1}{2M},0,1-\tfrac{2j-1}{2M}\bigr)$, whose first
coordinate is the midpoint of $I_j$. Then $p^{*}_1>r_1$ and
$p^{*}_3>1-r_2$ strictly, so $b_{\alpha^{(j)}}(\pvec^{*})=2$ by
\Cref{eq:hit-iff} under any tie-breaking rule, and $\pvec^{*}$ earns $0$ every
round. As $U_d\le 0$ everywhere by \Cref{eq:g3-payoff}, no coverage vector
does better, so $\pvec^{*}$ is a maximizer in \Cref{eq:pstar} and
\begin{equation}
\Regret(T)=\EE\bigl[\#\{t\le T:\text{miss at }t\}\bigr].
\label{eq:regret-is-misses}
\end{equation}
Regret counts missed rounds exactly.

\emph{The interaction is a comparison search.} A deterministic algorithm is a
family of maps $\pvec_t=\pvec_t(o_1,\dots,o_{t-1})$, the observations being
its only feedback. Take $q_t=\pvec_t$ and
$\mathcal{O}(\pvec,j')=b_{\alpha^{(j')}}(\pvec)$. The $I_j$ are disjoint, so
\Cref{lem:hit} gives exactly \Cref{eq:one-hit}.

\emph{Counting.} Every round before the first hit $\tau$ is a miss, so by
\Cref{eq:regret-is-misses} and \Cref{eq:count-final} with $M=2^{T}$,
\[
\EE_j\bigl[\Regret(T)\bigr]\ \ge\ \EE_j\bigl[\min\{\tau-1,T\}\bigr]
\ \ge\ T-\frac{2^{T+1}-T-2}{2^{T}},
\]
which is \Cref{eq:impossible}. Averaging over a randomized algorithm's
internal randomness preserves the bound, since it holds for every
deterministic realization.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[the bound is exactly tight]
\label{rem:tight-lb}
The constant cannot be improved: the defender that keeps the set of indices
consistent with its observations, an integer interval, by
\Cref{eq:type-line}, and plays $\pvec_t=(k/M,0,1-k/M)$ for its midpoint
$k$ attains expected regret exactly $T-2+(T+2)2^{-T}$, as
\Cref{sec:exp-imp} verifies. Since $\Regret(T)\le T$ always in $G_3$, the
defender loses all but two of the $T$ rounds.
\end{remark}

\Citet{letchford2009} show that a defender facing a \emph{single} unknown
attacker type can learn a near-optimal commitment from a small number of
best-response queries, which at first sight is in tension with
\Cref{thm:impossible}. There is none: any such bound must depend on the
geometry of the best-response regions, and the family $\Univ_M$ of
\Cref{eq:dyadic} drives the region that has to be located down to width
$1/M=2^{-T}$.

\subsection{A $\sqrt{T}$ floor as soon as $\Kmax\ge 2$}
\label{sec:floor}

For $\Kmax=1$ the problem is entirely a naming problem. On a fixed finite
universe $\Univ_M$ the version-space search of \Cref{rem:tight-lb} finishes
after at most $\lceil\log_2 M\rceil$ misses and hits in every round
thereafter, so the regret stops growing: it does not grow with $T$ at all,
and the linear bound of \Cref{thm:impossible} is driven purely by
$|\Univ|=\infty$. That is special to one type. With
two types the regret is $\Omega(\sqrt T)$ on the \emph{smallest possible}
universe, under \emph{full} information, and even if the defender is told $K$
in advance, because it must now track which of two known opponents is
currently more frequent, and no naming settles that.

\begin{proposition}[two types already cost $\sqrt{T}$]
\label{prop:sqrtT}
Consider $G_3$ with the two types $\alpha^{(1)}=\alpha_{0,1/2}$ and
$\alpha^{(2)}=\alpha_{1/2,1}$, so $|\Univ|=|K|=\Kmax=2$, and let
$a_1,\dots,a_T$ be drawn i.i.d.\ uniformly from $K$. Then every online
algorithm, randomized or deterministic, under either feedback model, and
even one told $K$ with its utility vectors at $t=0$, satisfies
\begin{equation}
\EE\bigl[\Regret(T)\bigr]\;\ge\;\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}
\label{eq:sqrtT}
\end{equation}
for every $T\ge 2$; consequently some fixed sequence forces at least this
much.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The two intervals $(0,\tfrac12]$ and $(\tfrac12,1]$ are disjoint, so by
\Cref{lem:hit} every $\pvec$ hits at most one type. Write
$N_i=|\{t:a_t=\alpha^{(i)}\}|$, so $N_1+N_2=T$.

\emph{Benchmark.} A fixed $\pvec$ earns $0$ when it hits and $-1$ otherwise,
so it earns at least $-\min\{N_1,N_2\}$, and this is attained:
$\pvec=(\tfrac14,0,\tfrac34)$ hits $\alpha^{(1)}$ in all $N_1$ of its rounds
by \Cref{eq:hit-iff}, and $(\tfrac34,0,\tfrac14)$ hits $\alpha^{(2)}$ in all
$N_2$ of its rounds. Hence the benchmark earns $-\min\{N_1,N_2\}$.

\emph{Algorithm.} Fix $t$ and condition on the history, which depends only on
$a_1,\dots,a_{t-1}$ and the algorithm's randomness, hence is independent of
$a_t$. The played $\pvec_t$ hits at most one type, and $a_t$ is that type
with probability $\tfrac12$, so $\Pr[\text{hit at }t]\le\tfrac12$ and the
algorithm earns at most $-T/2$ in expectation.

\emph{Combining.} As $\min\{N_1,N_2\}=\tfrac12(T-|N_1-N_2|)$,
\[
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;\ge\;\tfrac{T}{2}-\EE[\min\{N_1,N_2\}]
\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\EE\bigl[|N_1-N_2|\bigr],
\]
and $N_1-N_2$ is a simple random walk $S_T$, for which
$\EE|S_T|\ge\sqrt{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}$ (\Cref{app:walk}). Nothing here refers
to the feedback model or to knowledge of $K$: only that $\pvec_t$ cannot hit
both types and that $a_t$ is independent of the past.
\end{proof}

The bound is tight on this instance in a strong sense: $\Pr[\text{hit}]\le
\tfrac12$ is an \emph{equality} for any algorithm that plays inside one of
the two intervals, so every such algorithm, follow-the-leader included,
and one simply told $K$ included, has expected regret exactly
$\tfrac12\EE|N_1-N_2|=\sqrt{T/(2\pi)}(1+o(1))$. The remaining freedom buys
nothing; \Cref{fig:impossible}(b) shows the measured curve sitting on that
value.

So the transition at $\Kmax=2$ is not about how hard a type is to identify
but about whether identification is the whole problem. On the upper
side, \citet[Theorem 6.1]{balcan2015} with $k=M$, legitimate, since a
finite universe \emph{is} a known type set, gives regret
$O(\min\{T,T^{2/3}nM\log^{1/3}(nM)\})$ under partial information, while
\Cref{thm:main} gives $O(n\Kmax\sqrt{T\log(n\Kmax)})$ under full
information. Closing the gap to $\Omega(\sqrt T)$ is the finite-$M$ question
of \Cref{sec:conc2}.

\section{Block Revelation: Recovering Sublinear Regret}
\label{sec:block}

\Cref{thm:impossible} shows that under partial information, the defender must
receive some information about the identities of the attacker types. In
particular, observing only the attacked target is not enough: the defender
needs to know \emph{which types are present}.

This section studies a small and realistic way of providing this information.
Every $B$ rounds, an oracle gives the defender the \emph{set} of attacker
types seen so far, together with their utility vectors. Importantly, the
oracle does not reveal when a type attacked, how often it attacked, or
whether it will attack again. Thus, the defender still cannot determine
which type was responsible for any particular round.

This is important because identifying the attacker type from its observed
best responses is exactly the difficulty considered by
\citet[Section 6]{balcan2015}. Our additional information does not remove
this difficulty; it only tells the defender which types are present. As a
result, the problem remains harder than the full-information setting, and
the resulting regret rate is $T^{2/3}$ rather than $\sqrt{T}$.

\subsection{The model}

\begin{problem}[Unknown-$K$ repeated SSG with block revelation]
\label{prob:block}
Fix a \emph{revelation length} $B\in\NN$, known to the defender, and divide
the horizon into $N_B=\lceil T/B\rceil$ \emph{revelation blocks}
\begin{equation}
\cB_\nu
=
\{(\nu-1)B+1,\ \dots,\ \min\{\nu B,T\}\},
\qquad \nu\in[N_B].
\label{eq:blocks}
\end{equation}
Thus, $\cB_\nu$ is the $\nu$-th block of rounds, and a revelation occurs
at the end of each block. The game is \Cref{prob:partial} with the following
additional feedback: at the end of $\cB_\nu$, the defender receives
\begin{equation}
D_\nu
=
\{a_t:\ t\le \min\{\nu B,T\}\}
\subseteq K,
\label{eq:Dnu}
\end{equation}
where $a_t\in K$ denotes the attacker type that attacks in round $t$.
Thus, $D_\nu$ is the set of distinct attacker types that have appeared in
the first $\nu$ blocks. The defender also receives the utility vectors of
all types $\alpha\in D_\nu$. We set $D_0=\emptyset$. No other information
is revealed. Within each
round, the feedback remains partial.
\end{problem}




Three remarks delimit the model. It is strictly weaker than
\Cref{prob:unknownK}: even at $B=1$ the defender learns a round's type only
when that type is new. Taking $B\ge T$ makes the revelation useless and
recovers \Cref{prob:partial}, so by \Cref{thm:impossible} some bound on $B$
is necessary. And the oracle is
cumulative rather than incremental, but a defender that remembers $D_{\nu-1}$
can convert between the two, so we use whichever is convenient.

\subsection{The algorithm}

\Cref{alg:bgc} keeps the outer skeleton of \Cref{alg:gc}: maintain a
catalogue $C$ of the attacker types the defender has been told about,
run an inner regret-minimizer on $\cE(C;\epsilon)$, and restart the
inner learner whenever $C$ grows. Two changes are forced by the model.
First, the catalogue can enlarge only at the checkpoints $t=B,2B,\dots$,
so restarts happen at block boundaries rather than at the exact round a
new type first attacks. Second, Polynomial Weights needs the round-level
loss $-U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)$, which requires knowing $a_t$; under
partial information the defender does not see $a_t$, so this loss cannot
be computed. We therefore replace Polynomial Weights by the
partial-information learner $\mathcal{A}(C)$ of
\citet[Algorithm 1]{balcan2015}. We fix $\epsilon=1/T$: the approximation
error $2\epsilon L$ of \Cref{lem:43} only shrinks with $\epsilon$, and
\Cref{lem:44} does not involve $\epsilon$.

We treat $\mathcal{A}(C)$ as a black box. Its input is a catalogue $C$
(which fixes the expert set $\cE(C;\epsilon)$); at each round it receives
the observed target $i_t$ and outputs the strategy
$\pvec_t\in\cE(C;\epsilon)$ to play. Internally, $\mathcal{A}(C)$ groups
its rounds into estimation blocks and updates once per block, using each
block's observations to form an unbiased estimate of the average
per-strategy loss (via a barycentric-spanner probe on $|C|$ random rounds
of the block); that estimate is fed to a background full-information
regret minimizer whose output drives the next block. The regret guarantee
that this yields is the only property of $\mathcal{A}$ the analysis uses,
and we state and prove it inside the proof of \Cref{thm:block}, where it
is applied.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Block-Grow-Catalogue} (partial information, revelation length $B$).}
\label{alg:bgc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\small
\Require horizon $T$; revelation length $B$; game primitives; default strategy $\pvec_{\text{def}}\in\cP$
\State $C\gets\emptyset$;~$\epsilon\gets 1/T$;~$\mathcal{A}\gets\textsc{null}$
   \Comment{empty catalogue, no inner learner yet}
\For{$t=1,\dots,T$}
  \State \textbf{Play} $\pvec_t\gets\pvec_{\text{def}}$ if $C=\emptyset$, else the strategy $\mathcal{A}$ prescribes for round $t$
  \State \textbf{Observe} attacked target $i_t=b_{a_t}(\pvec_t)$
     \Comment{identity of $a_t$ not revealed}
  \State pass $i_t$ to $\mathcal{A}$ as its round-$t$ feedback
  \If{$t=\nu B$ for some $\nu$, or $t=T$} \Comment{revelation checkpoint}
    \State receive $D_\nu\subseteq K$ and the utilities of each $\alpha\in D_\nu$
    \If{$D_\nu\ne C$} \Comment{new type(s) appeared during $\cB_\nu$}
      \State $C\gets D_\nu$
      \State $\mathcal{A}\gets\mathcal{A}(C)$
         \Comment{restart on enlarged catalogue; state built during $\cB_\nu$ is discarded}
    \EndIf
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Block structure}

The analysis decomposes the horizon into two kinds of stretches: the
blocks whose closing checkpoint names at least one new type, and the runs
of blocks in between. Only the second kind carries useful learning; the
first is charged to the additive term. \Cref{fig:blocks} illustrates the
picture, and \Cref{def:blocks,lem:blocks} make it formal.

\label{fig:blocks}

\begin{definition}[discovery blocks and epochs]
\label{def:blocks}
Call $\cB_\nu$ a \emph{discovery block} if $D_\nu\supsetneq D_{\nu-1}$
and a \emph{clean block} otherwise. Let $\beta_1<\cdots<\beta_m$ index
the discovery blocks. The $j$-th \emph{epoch} $\Ep_j$ is the union of
the clean blocks strictly between the $j$-th and $(j{+}1)$-th discovery
block,
\begin{equation}
\Ep_j\;=\;\bigcup_{\nu=\beta_j+1}^{\beta_{j+1}-1}\cB_\nu,
\qquad L_j\coloneqq|\Ep_j|,
\label{eq:block-epoch}
\end{equation}
with $\beta_{m+1}\coloneqq N_B+1$. Write $C_j\coloneqq D_{\beta_j}$ for
the catalogue \Cref{alg:bgc} carries through $\Ep_j$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[decomposition]
\label{lem:blocks}
Let $K'=|\{a_1,\dots,a_T\}|\le\Kmax$ be the number of types that actually
appear in $\avec$. For every oblivious $\avec\in K^T$:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.1em,topsep=0.2em,label=(\alph*)]
  \item \emph{Partition.} $\beta_1=1$, $m\le K'$, and
  $[1,T]=\bigl(\bigsqcup_{j}\cB_{\beta_j}\bigr)\sqcup\bigl(\bigsqcup_{j}\Ep_j\bigr)$
  with $\sum_j L_j\le T$ and the discovery blocks contributing at most
  $mB\le K'B$ rounds in total.
  \item \emph{Clean epochs.} Throughout $\Ep_j$ the working catalogue
  equals $C_j$; every attacker that appears in $\Ep_j$ belongs to $C_j$;
  and \Cref{alg:bgc} runs one uninterrupted inner instance
  $\mathcal{A}(C_j)$ that was started at the first round of $\Ep_j$ and
  every observation of which \emph{at a round of $\Ep_j$} comes from a
  type inside $C_j$.
  \item \emph{Obliviousness.} The discovery blocks and the epochs are
  functions of $\avec$ alone, independent of the defender's plays and
  randomization.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\emph{(a)} $D_0=\emptyset\ne D_1$ makes $\cB_1$ a discovery block; each
discovery block adds at least one type, so $m\le K'$. Every block is
either a discovery block or lies in exactly one epoch, and holds $\le B$
rounds.

\emph{(b)} If $\cB_\nu$ is clean then $D_\nu=D_{\nu-1}$, so any
$t\in\cB_\nu$ has $a_t\in D_{\nu-1}$; no new type surfaces inside a clean
block. \Cref{alg:bgc} changes $C$ only at discovery blocks, so $C=C_j$
throughout $\Ep_j$, and by the same argument $a_t\in C_j$ for every
$t\in\Ep_j$. The instance is restarted at the checkpoint closing
$\beta_j$ and not again inside $\Ep_j$. The restriction ``at a round of
$\Ep_j$'' matters because the instance keeps running into the following
discovery block, where contaminated observations can enter its state,
but \Cref{alg:bgc} discards it at the next checkpoint, so no
contamination survives into a later epoch or enters the regret bound.

\emph{(c)} By \Cref{eq:Dnu}, each $D_\nu$ is a function of
$a_1,\dots,a_{\min\{\nu B,T\}}$; the same holds for the predicate
``$\cB_\nu$ is a discovery block'' and for the $\beta_j$ and epochs.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Main result}

\begin{theorem}[block revelation restores no-regret]
\label{thm:block}
Let the defender play \Cref{alg:bgc} in \Cref{prob:block}. For any oblivious
$\avec\in K^T$ with $|K|\le\Kmax$, and $K'\le\Kmax$ the number of distinct
types appearing,
\begin{equation}
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;\le\;
c\, n\,K'^{4/3}\,T^{2/3}\log^{1/3}(nK')\;+\;2K'B
\label{eq:block-main}
\end{equation}
for an absolute constant $c$; in particular
$\EE[\Regret(T)]=O(n\Kmax^{4/3}T^{2/3}\log^{1/3}(n\Kmax)+\Kmax B)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix an oblivious adversarial sequence $\avec\in K^T$ with $|K|\le\Kmax$
and let $K'$ be the number of distinct types that appear in $\avec$.
Adopt the notation of \Cref{def:blocks,lem:blocks}. For any set of rounds
$S\subseteq[1,T]$ write
\begin{equation}
\Regret(S)=\sum_{t\in S}U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})
-\EE\Bigl[\sum_{t\in S}U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)\Bigr],
\label{eq:regret-of-S}
\end{equation}
so that $\Regret([1,T])=\Regret(T)$ and $\Regret$ is additive over
disjoint unions. The proof proceeds in ten steps.

\paragraph{Step 1 (Block decomposition).}
By \Cref{lem:blocks}, the horizon splits as the disjoint union of the
$m\le K'$ discovery blocks $\cB_{\beta_j}$ and the $m$ epochs $\Ep_j$.
Additivity of \Cref{eq:regret-of-S} therefore gives
\begin{equation}
\Regret(T)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Regret(\Ep_j)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Regret(\cB_{\beta_j}).
\label{eq:block-split}
\end{equation}
Steps~2--5 control the first sum; Step~6 controls the second.

\paragraph{Step 2 (Catalogue is constant and correct inside an epoch).}
By \Cref{lem:blocks}, throughout $\Ep_j$ the working catalogue equals
$C_j\coloneqq D_{\beta_j}$, every attacker $a_t$ with $t\in\Ep_j$ lies in
$C_j$, and a single instance $\mathcal{A}(C_j)$ started at the first
round of $\Ep_j$ runs uninterrupted through the epoch on observations
drawn from $C_j$. Also by \Cref{lem:blocks}, the epoch and its indexing
are functions of $\avec$ alone, so the sub-sequence
$(a_t)_{t\in\Ep_j}$ is oblivious.

\paragraph{Step 3 (Regret guarantee for the inner learner).}
The only property of $\mathcal{A}(C)$ that the analysis uses is an
oblivious no-regret bound on the fixed-catalogue subproblem, the
partial-information counterpart of \Cref{prop:hedge}. Importing it from
\citet[Theorem 6.1]{balcan2015} needs one adjustment: their algorithm is
stated for a horizon $H$ known in advance, because it splits $[1,H]$
into a horizon-tuned number of \emph{estimation blocks} (each spending
$|C|$ rounds probing the barycentric spanner and the rest playing what a
background full-information sub-routine proposes), with the block count
chosen to balance exploration and exploitation at that particular $H$.
The epoch $\Ep_j$, by contrast, closes only when a new type appears,
so its length $L_j$ is unknown to $\mathcal{A}(C_j)$ when it starts.
We need a bound holding \emph{simultaneously} at every prefix
$L\le T$, with $L$ on the right-hand side.

\begin{lemma}[anytime partial-information learner]
\label{lem:anytime}
For every finite catalogue $C$ and every $\epsilon\le 1/T$ there is a
variant of $\mathcal{A}(C)$ that does not require its horizon in
advance, and an absolute constant $c_4$, such that on any oblivious
sequence $a'_1,a'_2,\dots$ with $a'_t\in C$ and for every $L\le T$
simultaneously,
\begin{align}
&\max_{\pvec\in\cP}\sum_{t=1}^{L}U_d(b_{a'_t}(\pvec),\pvec)
 -\EE\Bigl[\sum_{t=1}^{L}U_d(b_{a'_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)\Bigr]\notag\\
&\hspace{6em}\le\;c_4\,L^{2/3}\,n\,|C|\,\log^{1/3}(n|C|).
\label{eq:anytime}
\end{align}
The benchmark on the left is the best fixed strategy in hindsight over
$\cP$ on the sequence $a'_1,\dots,a'_L$, i.e.\ the epoch-local optimum;
passing to the global $\pvec^{*}$ of the theorem is Step~4b.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{lem:anytime}]
Two ingredients suffice: (i) Balcan's bound, though stated at the end of
a run tuned to a horizon $H$, is actually valid at every intermediate
prefix of that run; (ii) a doubling wrapper removes the tuning.

\emph{(i) Balcan's bound holds at every prefix.}
Fix $H$ and run $\mathcal{A}$ tuned to it. The core of
\citet[Lemma 6.2]{balcan2015}'s argument is a chain of inequalities
which, after any $\tau_0$ completed estimation blocks, bounds the
algorithm's expected loss so far by the loss of the best fixed strategy
over those same blocks plus two error terms: one that counts the
$|C|$ exploration rounds consumed per block, and one that carries the
regret of the background full-information sub-routine after $\tau_0$
estimated loss vectors. Both grow monotonically in $\tau_0$, and by
\Cref{prop:hedge} the sub-routine is itself anytime, so its regret
bound holds at every $\tau_0$, not only at Balcan's fully-tuned choice.
Reading the chain at any $\tau_0$ therefore reproduces their bound with
$L$ in place of $H$; a possibly-incomplete last block and the
discretization error $2\epsilon L\le 2$ from \Cref{lem:43} contribute a
constant that is absorbed. What fixes the exponent is the horizon-tuned
choice of block count: there is an absolute constant $c_0$ such that a
run tuned to $H$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\text{regret at prefix }L\;\le\;c_0\,H^{2/3}\,n|C|\log^{1/3}(n|C|)
\label{eq:prefix-bound}
\end{equation}
for every $L\le H$.
The right-hand side depends on $H$, not $L$: at short prefixes we are
still paying for the horizon we tuned to.

\emph{(ii) Doubling wrapper.}
To replace $H^{2/3}$ by $L^{2/3}$, run $\mathcal{A}$ in \emph{stages}
of exponentially growing length: stage $s=0,1,2,\dots$ has length
$2^{s}$ and is a fresh copy of Balcan's algorithm tuned to horizon
$2^{s}$; at the end of stage $s$ we discard its state and start stage
$s+1$. Fix any $L$ and let $S$ be the stage containing round $L$;
stages $0,\dots,S-1$ occupy $2^{S}-1$ rounds, all $\le L$, so
$2^{S}\le L$.

Regret is subadditive across stages: for any $\pvec\in\cP$ the payoff
over $[1,L]$ decomposes as a sum of per-stage payoffs, and
$\max_{\pvec\in\cP}$ of a sum is at most the sum of per-stage
$\max_{\pvec\in\cP}$'s. Subtracting the algorithm's payoff stage by
stage,
\[
\text{regret over }[1,L]\;\le\;\sum_{s=0}^{S}\text{regret of stage }s
\;\le\;c_0\,\phi\sum_{s=0}^{S}2^{2s/3},
\]
where $\phi\coloneqq n|C|\log^{1/3}(n|C|)$ and each stage's regret comes
from \Cref{eq:prefix-bound} with $H=2^{s}$. The remaining sum is a
geometric series with ratio $2^{2/3}>1$, so
\[
\sum_{s=0}^{S}2^{2s/3}\;\le\;\frac{2^{2/3}}{2^{2/3}-1}\cdot 2^{2S/3}
\;\le\;\frac{2^{2/3}}{2^{2/3}-1}\cdot L^{2/3},
\]
the last step using $2^{S}\le L$. Setting
$c_4\coloneqq 2^{2/3}c_0/(2^{2/3}-1)<2.71\,c_0$ yields
\Cref{eq:anytime}.
\end{proof}

The doubling wrapper costs a constant factor of $\approx 2.71$; the
exponent $2/3$ and every other factor in \Cref{eq:anytime} are
preserved.

\paragraph{Step 4a (Applying \Cref{lem:anytime} to an epoch).}
Fix $j$. By Step~2 the sub-sequence $(a_t)_{t\in\Ep_j}$ is oblivious and
takes values in $C_j$, so the hypotheses of \Cref{lem:anytime} are met
with catalogue $C_j$, prefix length $L_j$, and the sub-sequence in the
role of $a'_1,\dots,a'_{L_j}$. It yields
\begin{align}
&\underbrace{\max_{\pvec\in\cP}\!\sum_{t\in\Ep_j}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)}_{\eqqcolon\;\mathrm{OPT}_j}
 -\EE\!\Bigl[\sum_{t\in\Ep_j}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)\Bigr]\notag\\
&\hspace{4em}\le\;c_4\,L_j^{2/3}\,n\,|C_j|\,\log^{1/3}\!\bigl(n|C_j|\bigr).
\label{eq:balcan61-epoch}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Step 4b (Passing from $\mathrm{OPT}_j$ to $\pvec^{*}$).}
The bound \Cref{eq:balcan61-epoch} is against $\mathrm{OPT}_j$, the
\emph{epoch-local} maximum: the best cumulative utility on $\Ep_j$ over
\emph{any} $\pvec\in\cP$. The regret of \Cref{eq:regret-of-S}, on the
other hand, is measured against the global benchmark $\pvec^{*}$ defined
in \Cref{eq:pstar}, which is optimal over the full horizon $[1,T]$, not
over $\Ep_j$. The two disagree in general:
$\mathrm{OPT}_j\ne\sum_{t\in\Ep_j}U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})$,
because a strategy tuned to the specific mix of types active in
$\Ep_j$ can beat $\pvec^{*}$ on that epoch even though $\pvec^{*}$
dominates it over $[1,T]$.

The passage from the local $\mathrm{OPT}_j$ to the global $\pvec^{*}$ is
however immediate. Since $\pvec^{*}\in\cP$, it is a feasible competitor
in the maximum defining $\mathrm{OPT}_j$, so
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{OPT}_j\;\ge\;\sum_{t\in\Ep_j}U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*}).
\label{eq:opt-vs-pstar-block}
\end{equation}
The local-optimum bound of \Cref{eq:balcan61-epoch} therefore implies
a bound against the global benchmark, since $\pvec^{*}$ lives in the
same feasible set $\cP$ as the maximizer of $\mathrm{OPT}_j$.

\paragraph{Step 5 (Per-epoch combined bound).}
Combining \Cref{eq:balcan61-epoch,eq:opt-vs-pstar-block} with the
definition \Cref{eq:regret-of-S} gives, for every $j\in[m]$,
\begin{equation}
\Regret(\Ep_j)\;\le\;c_4\,L_j^{2/3}\,n\,|C_j|\,\log^{1/3}\!\bigl(n|C_j|\bigr).
\label{eq:combined-epoch-block}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 6 (Discovery blocks).}
Since $U_d(i,\pvec)\in[-1,1]$ for every target $i$ and every
$\pvec\in\cP$, each round contributes at most $2$ to
\Cref{eq:regret-of-S}. A discovery block holds at most $B$ rounds, and
by \Cref{lem:blocks} there are $m\le K'$ discovery blocks, so
\begin{equation}
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Regret(\cB_{\beta_j})\;\le\;2mB\;\le\;2K'B.
\label{eq:disc-block-sum}
\end{equation}
This is the price of batching: no learning is attempted inside a
discovery block, and \Cref{thm:blocklb} shows that no algorithm can do
better.

\paragraph{Step 7 (Uniform bound on the catalogue size).}
Since the catalogue only grows and the terminal catalogue $D_{\beta_m}$
contains at most $K'$ types, $|C_j|\le K'$ for every $j$. Hence
\begin{equation}
|C_j|\log^{1/3}(n|C_j|)\;\le\;K'\log^{1/3}(nK')\qquad\text{uniformly in }j,
\label{eq:uniform-Cj}
\end{equation}
which lets the catalogue-dependent factor be pulled out of the sum
in the next step. The size bound on $\cE(C_j;\epsilon)$ that would
otherwise appear here is already folded into the statement of
\Cref{lem:anytime}.

\paragraph{Step 8 (Summing across epochs).}
Substituting \Cref{eq:combined-epoch-block,eq:disc-block-sum,eq:uniform-Cj}
into \Cref{eq:block-split},
\begin{equation}
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;\le\;c_4\,n\,K'\log^{1/3}(nK')\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_j^{2/3}
\;+\;2K'B.
\label{eq:block-after-sum}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 9 (H\"older's inequality).}
The remaining sum $\sum_j L_j^{2/3}$ is controlled by H\"older's
inequality, which we recall. For real numbers
$x_1,\dots,x_m,y_1,\dots,y_m$ and conjugate exponents $p,q>1$ with
$1/p+1/q=1$,
\begin{equation}
\sum_{j=1}^{m}|x_j y_j|
\;\le\;\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{m}|x_j|^p\Bigr)^{1/p}
       \Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{m}|y_j|^q\Bigr)^{1/q}.
\label{eq:holder-stmt}
\end{equation}
Set $x_j=1$ and $y_j=L_j^{2/3}$, and take $p=3$, $q=3/2$; then
$\sum |x_j|^p=m$ and $\sum |y_j|^q=\sum L_j$, so
\begin{equation}
\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_j^{2/3}
\;\le\;m^{1/3}\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_j\Bigr)^{2/3}
\;\le\;K'^{1/3}\,T^{2/3},
\label{eq:holder}
\end{equation}
where the last step uses $m\le K'$ and $\sum_j L_j\le T$ from
\Cref{lem:blocks}. Equality in \Cref{eq:holder} holds when all epochs
have equal length, and this is where the multiplicative $K'^{1/3}$
factor of the theorem is generated.

\paragraph{Step 10 (Final substitution).}
Substituting \Cref{eq:holder} into \Cref{eq:block-after-sum} gives
\begin{equation*}
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;\le\;c_4\,n\,K'^{4/3}\,T^{2/3}\log^{1/3}(nK')+2K'B,
\end{equation*}
which is \Cref{eq:block-main} with $c=c_4$. Using $K'\le\Kmax$ gives the
$\Kmax$-form of the theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[reading the bound]
\label{rem:price}
\Cref{eq:block-main} differs from \citet[Theorem 6.1]{balcan2015} at
$k=\Kmax$ in two places. The multiplicative factor $\Kmax^{1/3}$ is the
price of learning $K$ \emph{incrementally}, produced by H\"older over the
epochs (analogous to the $\sqrt{\Kmax}$ that Cauchy--Schwarz produces in
\Cref{thm:main}). The additive $2\Kmax B$ is the price of batching,
tight up to a factor $2B/(B-2)$ by \Cref{thm:blocklb}. The exponent
$2/3$ is inherited from the inner learner and untouched by the unknown
catalogue.

Two consequences. First, taking $B=1$ does \emph{not} recover
\Cref{thm:main}: even then the defender still cannot attribute a round
to a specific known type, so the inner learner remains the
frequency-estimating one, which fixes the $2/3$ exponent regardless of
the decomposition. Second, \Cref{alg:bgc} never reads $\Kmax$: the
bound is stated in $K'$ and is instance-adaptive in the sense of
\Cref{cor:adaptive}, so the defender needs only $T$ and $B$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{How coarse may the revelation be?}

\Cref{eq:block-main} has two pieces: a learning term of order
$n\Kmax^{4/3}T^{2/3}\log^{1/3}(n\Kmax)$ and a batching term $2\Kmax B$.
The natural question is how large $B$ can be made before the second
overtakes the first.

\begin{corollary}[free batching, and the number of oracle calls]
\label{cor:B}
The batching term of \Cref{eq:block-main} is dominated as soon as $B\le B^{*}$, where
\begin{align}
B^{*}\;&=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,c\,n\,\Kmax^{1/3}\,T^{2/3}\log^{1/3}(n\Kmax)\notag\\
&=\;\Theta\!\bigl(n\Kmax^{1/3}T^{2/3}\log^{1/3}(n\Kmax)\bigr),
\label{eq:Bstar}
\end{align}
and then $\EE[\Regret(T)]=O(n\Kmax^{4/3}T^{2/3}\log^{1/3}(n\Kmax))$:
batching costs nothing at this rate. Equivalently, the defender need
consult the oracle only
\begin{equation}
N_B=\lceil T/B^{*}\rceil=
O\!\left(\frac{T^{1/3}}{n\,\Kmax^{1/3}\log^{1/3}(n\Kmax)}\right)
\label{eq:ncalls}
\end{equation}
times. Finally, \Cref{thm:block} is sublinear in $T$ iff
$n\Kmax^{4/3}\log^{1/3}(n\Kmax)=o(T^{1/3})$ and $\Kmax B=o(T)$; for
$\Kmax=O(1)$ and $B\le B^{*}$ the rate is $\tilde O(nT^{2/3})$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Solving $2\Kmax B\le c\,n\Kmax^{4/3}T^{2/3}\log^{1/3}(n\Kmax)$ for $B$
gives \Cref{eq:Bstar}, and \Cref{eq:ncalls} is $\lceil T/B^{*}\rceil$.
The sublinearity criterion is \Cref{eq:block-main} at $K'=\Kmax$,
divided by $T$, requiring each term to vanish.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The additive term is necessary}

The $\Theta(\Kmax B)$ term is not an artefact of the analysis but an
inherent cost of the model. The construction that certifies this reuses
$G_3$: each new type's threshold interval is nested strictly inside the
previous one's, so a single coverage vector, the midpoint of the
innermost interval, is optimal against all $\Kmax$ types
simultaneously, making the benchmark's regret zero. Every type must
nevertheless be located from scratch inside its block, since the oracle
reveals only that a type exists and provides its utility vector, not the
interval it prefers.

\begin{theorem}[a matching lower bound in $B$]
\label{thm:blocklb}
Let $G_3$ and $\Univ$ be as in \Cref{thm:impossible}. For every $B\ge 1$,
every $\Kmax\ge 1$ and every $T\ge \Kmax B$, there is a distribution over
instances of \Cref{prob:block} with $|K|=\Kmax$ on which every online
algorithm suffers
\begin{equation}
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;\ge\;\Kmax\,(B-2).
\label{eq:blocklb}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $M=2^{B}$, draw $k_1,\dots,k_{\Kmax}$ independently and uniformly from
$[M]$, and define nested intervals $J_0=(0,1]\supset J_1\supset\cdots$ by
letting $J_j$ be the $k_j$-th of the $M$ equal sub-intervals of $J_{j-1}$:
\begin{equation}
J_j=\Bigl(\ell_{j-1}+\tfrac{k_j-1}{M}|J_{j-1}|,\ \
        \ell_{j-1}+\tfrac{k_j}{M}|J_{j-1}|\Bigr],
\label{eq:nested}
\end{equation}
$\ell_{j-1}$ and $|J_{j-1}|$ being the left endpoint and length of
$J_{j-1}$. Let $\alpha^{[j]}$ be the threshold type with interval $J_j$, put
$K=\{\alpha^{[1]},\dots,\alpha^{[\Kmax]}\}$, and let $a_t=\alpha^{[j]}$ for
$t\in\cB_j$ and $a_t=\alpha^{[\Kmax]}$ for $t>\Kmax B$. The sequence is fixed
once the $k_j$ are drawn, so the adversary is oblivious, and each $J_j$ is a
\emph{proper} sub-interval of $J_{j-1}$, so the $\Kmax$ types are distinct.
As before we bound deterministic algorithms and appeal to Yao.

\emph{The benchmark is $0$.} Let $\mu$ be the midpoint of the innermost
interval $J_{\Kmax}$ and $\pvec^{*}=(\mu,0,1-\mu)$. Nesting gives
$r_1^{[j]}\le r_1^{[\Kmax]}<\mu$ and $1-r_2^{[j]}\le 1-r_2^{[\Kmax]}<1-\mu$
for every $j$, so $\pvec^{*}$ hits every type by \Cref{eq:hit-iff}, under any
tie-breaking rule, and regret again counts misses.

\emph{Each block is a fresh search.} When $\cB_j$ begins, the checkpoint
closing $\cB_{j-1}$ has revealed $\alpha^{[1]},\dots,\alpha^{[j-1]}$, hence
$J_{j-1}$, and nothing about $\alpha^{[j]}$, which has not yet attacked.
Moreover the whole transcript of $\cB_1,\dots,\cB_{j-1}$ is a function of
$k_1,\dots,k_{j-1}$ alone: inductively, each play is determined by earlier
observations, and each observation by that play and the interval then
attacking. As $k_j$ is drawn independently, it remains uniform on $[M]$
conditionally on everything the defender has seen and been told.

Condition on that transcript. Within $\cB_j$ the defender's plays are
deterministic functions of the observations made inside $\cB_j$, the only
attacker is $\alpha^{[j]}$, and its interval is one of $M$ disjoint
sub-intervals of $J_{j-1}$, so \Cref{lem:hit} gives \Cref{eq:one-hit}. The
conditioning has absorbed everything the oracle said before $\cB_j$, and it
says nothing during $\cB_j$, so within the block the interaction is a plain
protocol of the kind \Cref{lem:count} covers. \Cref{lem:count} with $M=2^{B}$
and horizon $B$ gives
$\EE[\#\text{misses in }\cB_j]\ge B-(2^{B+1}-B-2)/2^{B}>B-2$. Summing over
$j\in[\Kmax]$ and averaging over the algorithm's randomness gives
\Cref{eq:blocklb}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[the additive term is tight]
\label{rem:blocktight}
\Cref{thm:block,thm:blocklb} bracket the cost of batching between
$\Kmax(B-2)$ and $2\Kmax B$, so it is $\Theta(\Kmax B)$ for $B\ge 3$, the two
differing by $2B/(B-2)$, $4$ at $B=4$ and $2+o(1)$ thereafter; for $B\le 2$
the lower bound is vacuous. The two endpoints are worth naming. At $\Kmax=1$,
$B=T$ the bound reads $\EE[\Regret(T)]\ge T-2$, so \Cref{thm:impossible} is
exactly the $B\to T$ limit of \Cref{thm:blocklb}. At $B=1$ the additive term
is $2\Kmax$ and \Cref{eq:block-main} becomes
$O(n\Kmax^{4/3}T^{2/3}\log^{1/3}(n\Kmax)+\Kmax)$, the
partial-information counterpart of \Cref{thm:main}, and to our knowledge
the first sublinear bound for an unknown attacker set under
partial-information feedback.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Setup}

We implement \Cref{alg:gc} in Python 
, along
with two reference algorithms: the known-$K$ oracle of
\citet[Sec.~5]{balcan2015}, which receives the full set $K$ at $t=1$; and a
\emph{uniform} baseline that plays $\pvec_{\text{def}}=(R/n,\dots,R/n)$
every round. Unless stated otherwise, we use $n=3$ targets, $R=1$
resource, and attacker utilities drawn i.i.d.\ from $\mathrm{Unif}[-1,1]$
with $u_\alpha^u(i)>0>u_\alpha^c(i)$ to ensure covering is costly for the
attacker. The extreme-point set $\cE(C;\epsilon)$ is computed by direct
enumeration of intersections of $(n-1)$-subsets of the defining
hyperplanes, which is tractable at $n=3$.

We use a \emph{staggered-uniform} adversary: the horizon $[0,T)$ is
partitioned into $\Kmax$ phases of length $T/\Kmax$ each, and in phase
$i$ the attacker is drawn uniformly at random from the first $i$ types
$\{\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_i\}$. Thus $\alpha_1$ is present in every
phase, $\alpha_2$ from phase 2 onward, and so on. This design produces a
clean sequence of discovery rounds at $t=0,T/\Kmax,\dots,(\Kmax-1)T/\Kmax$,
while still mixing old and new types after each reveal so that the
benchmark $\pvec^{*}$ is a genuine compromise rather than a specialist
strategy.

The runs of \Cref{sec:exp-n3} were at $n=3$ targets, limited not by the

$\cE(C;\epsilon)$ explicitly, and building it by enumerating $(n-1)$-subsets
of the defining hyperplanes costs $7.3\times10^{14}$ linear solves at $n=10$.
Neither algorithm actually needs that set, however, each only needs to
\emph{optimize a linear function over it}, which one call to a
Bayesian-Stackelberg solver does. \Cref{app:oracle} gives the construction
and \Cref{app:bign} the resulting runs at $n=10$ and $n=20$; here we report
what they show and then test the lower bounds directly.

At $n=10$ and $n=20$ with $\Kmax=4$, \Cref{alg:gc} stays below the $C\sqrt t$
envelope of \Cref{thm:main} throughout, and \Cref{alg:bgc} below the
$Ct^{2/3}$ envelope of \Cref{thm:block}. Fitting the growth over
$T\in\{250,\dots,2000\}$ gives exponent $0.664$ for block revelation, the
$2/3$ of \Cref{thm:block}, to the resolution of the experiment, and
$0.317$ for full information, comfortably inside the $1/2$ of
\Cref{thm:main}. Sweeping $B$ at fixed $T$ gives regret growing linearly in
$B$ with slope $0.47$, well below the worst-case $2K'=8$, which is expected:
the worst case is attained only by the nested adversary of
\Cref{thm:blocklb}, exhibited separately in \Cref{fig:impossible}(c).

\subsection{The lower bounds, in simulation}
\label{sec:exp-imp}


\Cref{fig:impossible} plays the gadget $G_3$ of \Cref{sec:gadget} for real:
attacker types are built from the utility vectors of \Cref{eq:type-utils},
best responses are computed from those utilities, and the defender's payoff
is read off the attacked target. The defender is the optimal one for this
game, namely the midpoint version-space search of \Cref{rem:tight-lb},
and the expectation over the hidden type is computed exactly, by averaging
over all $M$ candidates rather than by sampling.

Panel (a) is \Cref{thm:impossible}: with $M=2^{T}$ the measured expected
regret equals $T-2+(T+2)2^{-T}$ for every $T\le 14$, to machine precision,
confirming both the lower bound and the claim in \Cref{rem:tight-lb} that it
is exactly attained.

Panel (b) is \Cref{prop:sqrtT}, and it is the one panel that shows a
$T$-dependence rather than a saturation. With $|K|=2$ on the two-element
universe, follow the leader has measured regret $1.57$, $6.35$ and $25.60$ at
$T=16$, $256$ and $4096$, against the exact optimum
$\tfrac12\EE|N_1-N_2|=1.571$, $6.377$, $25.531$ and the floor
$\tfrac12\sqrt{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}=1.41$, $5.66$, $22.63$. The dotted line is
the same universe with $|K|=1$, where the regret is $0.5$ at every horizon.
The two curves are the phase transition of \Cref{sec:floor}.

Panel (c) is \Cref{thm:blocklb}: on the nested-interval instance the measured
regret tracks $|K|(B-2)$ across $|K|\in\{1,2,3,4\}$ and
$B\in\{4,8,16,32\}$, so the additive $\Theta(\Kmax B)$ of \Cref{thm:block} is
real and not an artefact of the analysis.

\section{Discussion and Open Problems}
\label{sec:conc2}
\label{sec:conc}

We studied the repeated Stackelberg security game in a new, open regime
in which the set of attacker types is unknown to the defender a priori
but known to have bounded cardinality. We presented \textsc{Grow-Catalogue},
a simple online algorithm that incrementally discovers attacker types and
re-partitions the coverage polytope accordingly, and we gave a full proof
that its expected regret under full-information feedback is at most
$O\!\bigl(n\Kmax\sqrt{T\log (n\Kmax)}\bigr)$. This is exactly the known-$K$
bound of \citet[Theorem 5.1]{balcan2015} with the known cardinality $k$
replaced by our known upper bound $\Kmax$, inflated by the $\sqrt{\Kmax}$ that
the epoch decomposition costs.
The bound is sublinear whenever $n^2\Kmax^2\log(n\Kmax)=o(T)$, and
the matching $\Omega(T)$ lower bound of \citet[Theorem 7.1]{balcan2015}
rules out further improvements on the unbounded side. Our experiments
confirm the analytical scaling: the empirical regret stays below the
$C\sqrt T$ envelope and tracks the epoch structure of the proof.

Beyond that full-information picture, \Cref{sec:partial,sec:block} locate
the boundary of learnability for the
unknown-$K$ repeated security game. On one side, \Cref{thm:main}: an unknown
attacker set of bounded size costs only $\sqrt{\Kmax}$, provided the defender
is told after each round which type attacked. On the other,
\Cref{thm:impossible}: told nothing but the attacked target, every algorithm
loses all but two of the $T$ rounds, not because the adversary has many
types, but because the defender cannot name the one it faces. \Cref{tab:landscape} places this among the
known results.

Between the two lies \Cref{prob:block}, where an oracle periodically reports
the \emph{set} of types met so far, a list of names, nothing about when or
how often. \Cref{thm:block} shows this suffices: \Cref{alg:bgc} attains
$O(n\Kmax^{4/3}T^{2/3}\log^{1/3}(n\Kmax)+\Kmax B)$, the partial-information
rate of \citet[Theorem 6.1]{balcan2015} degraded by $\Kmax^{1/3}$ for the
unknown catalogue and by an additive $\Kmax B$ for the batching, the latter
tight by \Cref{thm:blocklb}. \Cref{cor:B} makes this a deployment statement:
the report needs to arrive only $\tilde O(T^{1/3})$ times over a horizon of
length $T$, and, by the matching bound there, not fewer.

What \Cref{thm:blocklb} isolates is a \emph{latency} cost: an oracle
that speaks only every $B$ rounds still costs $\Theta(\Kmax B)$, because
the defender plays blind between a type's arrival and the report naming
it. This places the additive term alongside the literature on online
learning under delayed feedback \citep{joulani2013}, with the difference
that here the delay applies not to the loss but to the \emph{identity}
of the opponent, and that \Cref{thm:impossible} makes the undelayed
identity indispensable rather than merely helpful.

Three questions are left open.

\paragraph{Is the exponent $2/3$ necessary here?}
The exponent in \Cref{thm:block} is inherited from
\citet[Theorem 6.1]{balcan2015} and untouched by the unknown catalogue, so
improving it would improve their result. Whether $T^{2/3}$ is optimal for
partial-information feedback even with $K$ known is not known; the natural
attack replaces their explore-then-exploit estimator by importance-weighted
estimates on the exploit rounds, which needs a version of the
clean-epochs part of \Cref{lem:blocks} for a continuously updated estimator.

\paragraph{Is the factor $\Kmax^{1/3}$ necessary?}
It comes from H\"older over the epochs, exactly as the $\sqrt{\Kmax}$ of
\Cref{thm:main} comes from Cauchy--Schwarz. \Cref{thm:blocklb} bounds only
the additive term and \Cref{prop:sqrtT} only the $T$-dependence; a bound
$\Omega(\Kmax^{c}T^{2/3})$ matching the multiplicative price is open under
both feedback models. The obvious attempt, $\Kmax/2$ disjoint copies of
the two-type construction of \Cref{prop:sqrtT}, one per epoch, fails
instructively. Per-epoch lower bounds do not add, because the benchmark
\Cref{eq:pstar} is a \emph{single} fixed coverage vector: with all intervals
disjoint, \Cref{lem:hit} says no $\pvec$ hits more than one type, so the
global benchmark is far weaker than the sum of the per-epoch ones and the
global regret correspondingly smaller. A matching lower bound must let the
epochs share a good comparator, as the nested family of \Cref{thm:blocklb}
does, while still forcing fresh learning in each.

The complementary attack is on the upper bound: avoid the restart and both
factors go with it, since without epochs there is neither a Cauchy--Schwarz
nor a H\"older step. Algorithms tracking a \emph{growing} expert set
\citep{mourtada2017} do that, and \Cref{sec:related} already notes them as a
drop-in for the Polynomial-Weights sub-routine. But replacing the sub-routine
is not enough: Steps~4 and 5 of \Cref{sec:proof} also use the epoch structure
to pass from the epoch-local optimum to the global $\pvec^{*}$. Under partial
information it is worse still, the estimator of \Cref{lem:lift}(ii) is
defined relative to a fixed catalogue, its spanner and ambient set both
changing when $C$ grows, so estimates from before and after a discovery block
are not estimates of comparable quantities.

\paragraph{Confounded search: $\Kmax\ge 2$ on a finite universe.}
For $\Kmax=1$ the picture is complete: on a resolution-$M$ universe a miss
halves the version space unambiguously, so \Cref{rem:tight-lb} gives an
optimum of $\Theta(\min\{T,\log_2 M\})$. For $\Kmax\ge 2$ the defender does
not know which opponent produced the observation, so the same search is
corrupted. \Cref{prop:sqrtT} settles one half, the answer must contain
$\Omega(\sqrt T)$, already at $M=2$, and the two upper bounds we can write
down, $O(n\Kmax\sqrt{T\log (n\Kmax)})$ and $O(T^{2/3}nM\log^{1/3}(nM))$,
contain no $\log M$ while the lower bound contains no $M$ at all. The joint
dependence is open.

\section{The random-walk bound of \Cref{prop:sqrtT}}
\label{app:walk}

Let $S_T=\sum_{t\le T}\varepsilon_t$ with $\varepsilon_t=\pm1$ independent
and uniform. We claim $\EE|S_T|\ge\sqrt{\lfloor T/2\rfloor}$. Since $|S_m|$
is a submartingale, $\EE|S_T|\ge\EE|S_{2\nu}|$ for $\nu=\lfloor T/2\rfloor$,
and the classical identity $\EE|S_{2\nu}|=2\nu\binom{2\nu}{\nu}4^{-\nu}$
reduces the claim to
\begin{equation}
\binom{2\nu}{\nu}4^{-\nu}\;\ge\;\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\nu}} .
\label{eq:centralbinom}
\end{equation}
This holds with equality at $\nu=1$ and follows by induction: the ratio of
consecutive left-hand sides is $(2\nu+1)/(2\nu+2)$ and of right-hand sides
$\sqrt{\nu/(\nu+1)}$, and $(2\nu+1)^2(\nu+1)\ge\nu(2\nu+2)^2$ reduces to
$\nu+1\ge 0$. Hence $\EE|S_T|\ge 2\nu/(2\sqrt\nu)=\sqrt{\nu}$.

\section{An oracle-efficient implementation}
\label{app:oracle}


For a catalogue $C$ and $\pvec\in\cP$ define the \emph{lift}
\begin{equation}
y_{\alpha,i}(\pvec)=\mathbb{I}\{b_\alpha(\pvec)=i\},\qquad
w_{\alpha,i}(\pvec)=y_{\alpha,i}(\pvec)\,p_i
\label{eq:lift}
\end{equation}
for $(\alpha,i)\in C\times N$, and let
$\mathcal{S}(C)=\{(y(\pvec),w(\pvec)):\pvec\in\cP\}\subseteq\RR^{2|C|n}$.

\begin{lemma}[both algorithms are linear over the lift]
\label{lem:lift}
Assume the types in $C$ have pairwise distinct best-response maps, so that
the set $\mathcal{W}=\{\mathbb{I}_{(\sigma=i)}\}$ of
\citet[Section 6.3]{balcan2015} has rank $|C|$ and its barycentric spanner
$\Lambda$ is invertible. Let $\omega\in\RR^{C}$ be any vector of weights on
the catalogue. Then
\begin{align}
\sum_{\alpha\in C}\omega_\alpha\,U_d(b_\alpha(\pvec),\pvec)
=\sum_{\alpha\in C}\sum_{i\in N}\Bigl[&\omega_\alpha u_d^u(i)\,y_{\alpha,i}(\pvec)\notag\\
+\;&\omega_\alpha\bigl(u_d^c(i)-u_d^u(i)\bigr)w_{\alpha,i}(\pvec)\Bigr],
\label{eq:lift-linear}
\end{align}
which is a linear function of the lift. Moreover:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=0.05em,topsep=0.2em]
  \item the loss used by \Cref{alg:gc} at round $t$ is
  $\ell_t(\pvec)=-U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)$, i.e.\ \Cref{eq:lift-linear} with
  $\omega=-\mathbb{I}_{\{a_t\}}$;
  \item the estimator $\hat c_\tau$ of \citet[Eq.~2 and Lemma
  6.4]{balcan2015}, used inside \Cref{alg:bgc}, is \Cref{eq:lift-linear} with
  $\omega=g_\tau$, and the loss it induces is its negation, i.e.\
  $\omega=-g_\tau$, where $g_\tau=\Lambda^{-1}\hat p_\tau$, $\Lambda$ is the
  matrix whose rows are the barycentric spanner $b_1,\dots,b_{|C|}$ of
  \citet[Section 6.3]{balcan2015} and $\hat p_\tau\in\{0,1\}^{|C|}$ collects
  that block's exploration outcomes. The vector $g_\tau$ is an unbiased
  estimator of the per-round type frequencies in the block.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For each $\alpha$ exactly one of $y_{\alpha,1},\dots,y_{\alpha,n}$ equals $1$,
so $U_d(b_\alpha(\pvec),\pvec)=\sum_i y_{\alpha,i}(\pvec)U_d(i,\pvec)$. By
\Cref{eq:Ud},
\begin{align*}
y_{\alpha,i}U_d(i,\pvec)
&=u_d^c(i)\,y_{\alpha,i}p_i+u_d^u(i)\,y_{\alpha,i}(1-p_i)\\
&=u_d^u(i)y_{\alpha,i}+\bigl(u_d^c(i)-u_d^u(i)\bigr)w_{\alpha,i},
\end{align*}
and summing against $\omega$ gives \Cref{eq:lift-linear}. Item (i) is the
special case $\omega=-\mathbb{I}_{\{a_t\}}$.

For (ii), recall that \citet[Eq.~2]{balcan2015} sets
$\hat c_\tau(\pvec)=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\lambda_j(\mathbb{I}_{(\sigma=i)})\,
\hat p_\tau(b_j)\,U_d(i,\pvec)$, where $\sigma$ is the response profile of
$\pvec$ and $\lambda(w)$ is the (unique, since $\Lambda$ is invertible)
vector of coefficients with $\sum_j\lambda_j(w)b_j=w$, i.e.\
$\lambda(w)^{\!\top}\Lambda=w^{\!\top}$. Hence
$\sum_j\lambda_j(w)\hat p_\tau(b_j)=\lambda(w)\cdot\hat p_\tau
= w^{\!\top}\Lambda^{-1}\hat p_\tau=w\cdot g_\tau$. The $\alpha$-th coordinate
of $\mathbb{I}_{(\sigma=i)}$ is exactly $y_{\alpha,i}(\pvec)$, so
\begin{align*}
\hat c_\tau(\pvec)
&=\sum_{i}\Bigl(\sum_\alpha g_{\tau,\alpha}y_{\alpha,i}(\pvec)\Bigr)U_d(i,\pvec)\\
&=\sum_{\alpha}g_{\tau,\alpha}\,U_d(b_\alpha(\pvec),\pvec),
\end{align*}
which is \Cref{eq:lift-linear} with $\omega=g_\tau$. Finally, \citet[Lemma 6.3]{balcan2015} gives
$\EE[\hat p_\tau(b_j)]=f_\tau(b_j)/|B_\tau|$ and $f_\tau(w)=w\cdot m_\tau$
with $m_\tau$ the vector of type counts in the block, so
$\EE[\hat p_\tau]=\Lambda m_\tau/|B_\tau|$ and
$\EE[g_\tau]=m_\tau/|B_\tau|$.
\end{proof}

\Cref{lem:lift} says that both algorithms are instances of online linear
optimization over $\mathcal{S}(C)$, whose single primitive is
\begin{equation}
\textsc{Oracle}(A,\Delta)=\argmax_{\pvec\in\cP}\ \sum_{\alpha,i}
\bigl(A_{\alpha,i}\,y_{\alpha,i}(\pvec)+\Delta_{\alpha,i}\,w_{\alpha,i}(\pvec)\bigr).
\label{eq:oracle}
\end{equation}
We therefore replace the Polynomial-Weights sub-routine of
\Cref{prop:hedge} by Follow the Perturbed Leader over $\mathcal{S}(C)$, which
calls \Cref{eq:oracle} once per round, and no times at all on the
exploration rounds of \Cref{alg:bgc}, which play a fixed spanner strategy. This is a change of
implementation and not of algorithm: \citet[Section 3.1]{balcan2015}
explicitly allow any black box satisfying their Proposition 1, and name
Follow the Lazy Leader of \citet{kalai2005} as an admissible choice. We run
it with the perturbation schedule of \citet{kalai2005}, in its anytime form
because the segment lengths are not known in advance. The three problem
constants that schedule needs take, on $\mathcal{S}(C)$, the values
$\|Y-Y'\|_1\le 4|C|$ for the $\ell_1$-diameter of the decision set,
$|Y\cdot s|\le 1$ for the per-round loss, and $\|s\|_1\le 3n$ for the
$\ell_1$-norm of a one-round loss vector, the last because
$\sum_i(|u_d^u(i)|+|u_d^c(i)-u_d^u(i)|)\le 3n$.

Grouping \Cref{eq:oracle} by the response profile $\sigma:C\to N$ makes the
objective linear on each cell $\cP_\sigma(C)$ of \Cref{eq:Psigma}, so
\Cref{eq:oracle} is a maximum of $n^{|C|}$ linear programs in $n$ variables
, polynomial in $n$ for a fixed catalogue, and the natural extension to a
set of types of the multiple-LP method of \citet{conitzer2006}. In the experiments we instead solve one
mixed-integer program with $n|C|$ binaries in the style of the
Bayesian-Stackelberg MILP of \citet{paruchuri2008}, using the profile
enumeration as a correctness reference. We do not claim polynomial time in
$|C|$: the Bayesian Stackelberg problem is NP-hard \citep{conitzer2006}. The
claim is polynomial time in $n$, which is the parameter that made
$\cE(C;\epsilon)$ unusable. Both back-ends solve over cells shrunk by a
strictness margin $\gamma=10^{-6}$, so that the returned $\pvec$ realizes its
profile under any tie-breaking rule; this plays the role of the
$\epsilon$-approximation in \Cref{lem:43}. The rank hypothesis of
\Cref{lem:lift} is not an extra assumption on the game but a normalisation of
the catalogue: two types with the same best-response map on all of $\cP$ are
indistinguishable to the defender's payoff, to the benchmark
\Cref{eq:pstar} and to the feedback, so \Cref{alg:bgc} keeps one
representative of each best-response map in $C$ and discards the duplicates.
This can only shrink the catalogue, so $|C_j|\le K'$ and every bound of
\Cref{sec:block} is unaffected; without it $\mathcal{W}$ can have rank below
$|C|$ and $\Lambda^{-1}$ in \Cref{lem:lift}(ii) need not exist. The
implementation does not de-duplicate but detects the failure: if the sampled
pool admits no full-rank spanner it records a rank failure for that catalogue
and skips exploration until the catalogue next changes. Over the runs behind
\Cref{fig:block} no rank failure occurred, which is what one expects of
independently drawn utility vectors. As in \Cref{sec:exp}, the
experiments take $\cP=\{\pvec\ge 0:\sum_i p_i=1\}$ with one resource; the
algorithm and the benchmark $\pvec^{*}$ optimize over the same set, so the
regret they define is the one of \Cref{eq:regret}. Every non-asymptotic claim of \Cref{sec:partial,sec:block}, together with the
oracle and estimator claims of this subsection, is re-checked by
\texttt{code/verify\_theory.py}, which prints a pass or fail for each; the
measured quantities of \Cref{sec:exp-tract,sec:exp-full,sec:exp-block,sec:exp-imp}
, fitted exponents, envelope constants and timings, are printed by the
plotting and benchmarking scripts that produce the corresponding figure or
table, and are reproduced by rerunning those.

One further gap between \Cref{alg:bgc} and its implementation should be
stated. The set $\mathcal{W}=\{\mathbb{I}_{(\sigma=i)}\}$ of
\citet[Section 6.3]{balcan2015} has one element per pair (cell, target) and
we do not enumerate it; instead we sample coverage vectors, read off the
vectors $\mathbb{I}_{(\sigma=i)}$ they realize together with their witnesses
$(\pvec_b,i_b)$, and run the greedy determinant maximization of
\citet{awerbuch2008} on that pool. The consequence is confined to the
\emph{range} of the estimator: a spanner of a sub-pool need not give
coefficients $\lambda\in[-1,1]$ for every $w\in\mathcal{W}$, so the bound
$\hat c_\tau\in[-n|C|,n|C|]$ of \citet[Lemma 6.4]{balcan2015} is not
guaranteed by the implementation. Unbiasedness, \Cref{lem:lift}(ii),
which is what the estimator's correctness rests on, needs only that
$\Lambda$ be invertible, and holds exactly; it is the check reported in the
previous paragraph. For the same reason the implementation does not scale the
Follow-the-Perturbed-Leader perturbation by that worst-case range: inside
\Cref{alg:bgc} it uses the schedule $\epsilon_t=\sqrt{4|C|/(3nt)}$ listed
above, which is what the range of the estimates we actually compute warrants,
rather than the range $n|C|$ that \citet[Lemma 6.4]{balcan2015} proves in the
worst case. This is a tuning choice and not a claim: \Cref{thm:block} rests on
\Cref{lem:anytime} and on their Lemma 6.4, not on the constants used here, and
what \Cref{fig:block} sets out to test is whether the predicted
\emph{exponent} appears in a run. Over the $60$ random instances with
$n\in\{3,4,5\}$ and $|C|\in\{1,2,3\}$ that \texttt{verify\_theory.py} runs,
the two back-ends and a brute-force maximization over the enumerated
$\cE(C;\epsilon)$ agree to within $1.4\times10^{-6}$, and a longer run of
$150$ instances does not push that figure up. We also
checked \Cref{lem:lift}(ii) directly: over the $4\times10^{4}$ replications
of a single estimation block at $n=6$, $|C|=3$ that the same script runs, the
mean of $|B_\tau|\,g_\tau$ reproduced the true type counts of the block to
within $0.05$ out of $40$, comfortably inside the three-standard-error band
of $1.1$ that Monte-Carlo noise allows; a separate run at $2\times10^{5}$
replications gave a largest error of $0.09$ against a standard error of
$0.16$, which is what an unbiased estimator should do.

and $T=1500$. Panels (a) and (b) reproduce \Cref{fig:per-round} under the
staggered-uniform adversary: the per-round regret spikes shortly after each
of the four phase boundaries, a new type becomes available at a boundary
and is first drawn a few rounds later, which is the discovery round, and
then decays inside the epoch that follows, exactly the structure that
Steps~1--6 of the proof of \Cref{thm:main} describe. Panels (c) and (d)
reproduce \Cref{fig:scaling} under the uniform-random adversary: the
cumulative regret stays below the $C\sqrt t$ envelope of \Cref{thm:main}
throughout, with $C=1.27$ at $n=10$ and $C=1.71$ at $n=20$, and it is
$8.0$ and $11.6$ times smaller than the uniform baseline.

We keep the two adversaries separate for the same reason \Cref{sec:exp} does,
and it is worth spelling out. The staggered-uniform adversary is
non-stationary by design, the type mixture changes at every phase boundary
, so the best \emph{fixed} strategy in hindsight of \Cref{eq:pstar} is a
weak comparator, and an algorithm that tracks the current phase can and does
beat it. At $n=10$ the cumulative regret against $\pvec^{*}$ came out
negative on one of the four seeds ($-35.3$), and the four-seed mean was
$-2.6$. That says nothing against \Cref{thm:main},
which is an upper bound, but it makes a cumulative-regret plot on that
adversary uninformative. The uniform-random adversary is stationary, so
$\pvec^{*}$ is the right comparator, and panels (c) and (d) use it. The
benchmark $\pvec^{*}$ is itself computed with one oracle call, so even
\emph{evaluating} the regret at $n=20$ was out of reach before
\Cref{app:oracle}.

\subsection{Block revelation at $n=10$ and $n=20$}
\label{sec:exp-block}

\Cref{fig:block} runs \Cref{alg:bgc} under genuine partial-information
feedback: the simulator passes the algorithm the attacked target only, and at
the end of each revelation block calls it with the \emph{set} of types seen
so far and nothing else. The set is handed over as a list for convenience,
but the algorithm reads it only through membership tests, so neither the
order in which the types first attacked nor any frequency information can
leak through it. Panels (a) and (b) show cumulative regret against
the $Ct^{2/3}$ envelope of \Cref{thm:block} under the staggered-uniform
adversary, which is the interesting one here because it spreads the discovery
blocks across the horizon and so produces the $\Theta(\Kmax)$ epochs that
Step~8 of the proof pays for; the envelope constants are $C=1.72$ at $n=10$
and $C=1.25$ at $n=20$.

Panel (c) sweeps the revelation length $B$ over six doublings at fixed $T$.
The measured growth is linear in $B$, as \Cref{thm:block,thm:blocklb}
predict, a least-squares fit over $B\in[4,256]$ has slope $0.47$, but
that slope is far below the worst-case $2K'=8$, and the panel draws both.
The gap is expected rather than a discrepancy: the worst case is attained
only by the nested adversary of \Cref{thm:blocklb}, which
\Cref{fig:impossible}(c) exhibits separately, and on a random instance most
of the rounds of a discovery block are not in fact wasted, because a strategy
tuned to the types already in the catalogue is usually not terrible against a
new one.

Panel (d) compares the two feedback models as $T$ grows, on the stationary
uniform-random adversary for the reason given in \Cref{sec:exp-full}. Over
$T\in\{250,500,1000,2000\}$ the block-revelation regret grows with fitted
exponent $0.664$, which is $2/3$ to within the resolution of the experiment
and is exactly the exponent of \Cref{thm:block}; the full-information regret
grows with exponent $0.317$, comfortably inside the $1/2$ of \Cref{thm:main}.
The asymmetry is not an accident of the instances. \Cref{alg:bgc} pays a
floor of $|C|$ exploration rounds in every estimation block whatever the
sequence does, so its $T^{2/3}$ is visible even on an easy sequence, whereas
\Cref{alg:gc} has no such floor and, as already observed in \Cref{sec:exp},
runs well inside its worst case against a stochastic adversary.

One implementation choice is worth stating, because it is where asymptotics
and simulation disagree. \Citet[Algorithm 1]{balcan2015} uses
$Z=n\bigl(T^{2}\log(n|C|)\bigr)^{1/3}$ estimation blocks, i.e.\ blocks of
$T/Z=T^{1/3}/\bigl(n\log^{1/3}(n|C|)\bigr)$ rounds, which at $n=10$, $|C|=4$
and $T=2000$ is less than one round, fewer, certainly, than the $|C|=4$
exploration rounds a block must hold, since the tuning of \citet[Theorem
6.1]{balcan2015} is asymptotic. We therefore use blocks of
$\max\{|C|+1,\lceil T^{1/3}\rceil\}$ rounds, which has the same order in
$T$. At $T=2000$ and $\Kmax=4$ that is $13$ rounds, of which $4$ are spent
exploring, so roughly $31\%$ of all rounds are exploration; this is the main
reason the measured regret in panels (a) and (b) is an order of magnitude
above the full-information regret of \Cref{fig:tractable} at the same
horizon, and it is a constant-factor effect, not a change of exponent.
