\section{Abstract}
\label{sec:Abstract}
Stackelberg Security Games (SSGs) model the strategic allocation of limited
defender resources against attackers whose preferences are often uncertain.
\citet{balcan2015} designed no-regret online learning algorithms for the
\emph{repeated} variant of SSGs against an adversarial sequence of attackers
drawn from a finite, \emph{known} set $K$, and proved a matching linear-regret
lower bound when $K$ is unbounded. A natural real-world middle ground is
left open: the defender does not know $K$ a priori, but has a bound $\Kmax$
on its size. We study this setting in the full-information feedback model.
We propose \textsc{Grow-Catalogue}, an algorithm that incrementally learns
new attacker types as they arrive, re-partitions the coverage polytope, and
runs an anytime multiplicative-weights sub-routine on the resulting
extreme-point set. Our central technical contribution is a full proof that
\textsc{Grow-Catalogue} achieves expected regret
\[
O\!\left(\sqrt{T\cdot\Kmax\cdot(n^2+\Kmax\log n)}\right)
\]
against any oblivious adversarial sequence of length $T$. The rate matches
that of \citet{balcan2015} with $k$ replaced by $\Kmax$ and is sublinear
whenever $\Kmax\cdot(n^2+\Kmax\log n)=o(T)$. Experiments on synthetic SSG
instances confirm the predicted $\sqrt{T}$ scaling, and the empirical regret
stays comfortably below the analytic $C\sqrt{T}$ envelope. Our implementation and all experiment scripts are at
\textcolor{blue}{\href{https://github.com/aditisingh1053/Security_Games}{github.com/aditisingh1053/Security\_Games}}.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In a Stackelberg Security Game (SSG), a defender allocates a scarce set of
resources to protect $n$ potential targets against a strategic attacker
who, observing the defender's randomized deployment, chooses the best
target to attack \citep{tambe2012}. Deployed systems built around this model
include the US Coast Guard's port protection patrols, the Federal Air
Marshals assignment system, the LAX airport patrol schedule, and
anti-poaching patrols in several African national parks
\citep{tambe2012,yang2014}. In every one of these applications the
defender must commit to its deployment before the attack happens, and the
attacker's payoff function encodes its preferences over targets.

The \emph{classical} SSG analysis assumes that the attacker's payoff
function is fully known to the defender. The optimal commitment can then
be computed by a linear program \citep[e.g.,][]{pita2010}. In practice,
however, the defender faces considerable uncertainty about the attacker.
There may be several distinct threat actors (e.g., different poacher
groups, different types of smugglers), and their utilities are only
imperfectly known. A principled response, pioneered by \citet{balcan2015},
is to drop the single-type assumption entirely and instead ask for a
\emph{no-regret} online algorithm against an adversarially chosen sequence
of attacker types, drawn from a known finite set $K$ with $|K|=k$. Under
full-information feedback, they design an algorithm whose expected
cumulative regret is bounded by $O(\sqrt{Tn^2 k\log(nk)})$, which is
sublinear in the horizon $T$, and they prove a matching linear-regret lower
bound when $|K|$ is unrestricted \citep[Theorem~7.1]{balcan2015}.

The assumption that the set $K$ itself is known to the defender in advance
is, however, a strong one. In realistic deployments the defender typically
does \emph{not} have a complete list of attacker types at the start. What
it may have is an \emph{upper bound} $\Kmax$ on the number of distinct
types it expects to encounter (for instance, from threat intelligence or
from physical constraints of the domain). The question that motivates this
project is therefore:
\begin{center}
\emph{Can the defender achieve sublinear regret when $K$ is unknown a
priori, subject to $|K|\le \Kmax$?}
\end{center}
This question lies squarely in the gap left open between the
\emph{known-$K$} upper bound and the \emph{unrestricted-$K$} lower bound of
\citet{balcan2015}. It is not answered by any of the existing work, and
resolving it is the main goal of the present report.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contrib}

Our contributions are the following.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em,itemsep=0.10em,topsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Problem formalization (\Cref{sec:unknownK}).}
    We formalize the unknown-$K$ variant of the repeated Stackelberg
    security game, identifying the minimal modification of the model of
    \citet{balcan2015} that captures it.
  \item \textbf{Algorithm (\Cref{sec:alg}).}
    We propose \textsc{Grow-Catalogue}, an online algorithm that at each
    round maintains a \emph{catalogue} $C_t\subseteq K$ of attacker types
    observed so far, recomputes the set $\cE(C_t;\epsilon)$ of
    $\epsilon$-approximate extreme points of the partition of $\cP$
    induced by $C_t$, and runs an anytime multiplicative-weights
    sub-routine over $\cE(C_t)$. When a new type is observed, the
    catalogue is updated and the sub-routine is restarted on the enlarged
    expert set.
  \item \textbf{Main theorem and a complete, detailed proof
    (\Cref{sec:mainresult,sec:proof}).}
    The technical core of this report is a self-contained proof that
    \textsc{Grow-Catalogue} achieves expected regret at most
    $O(\sqrt{T\cdot\Kmax\cdot(n^2+\Kmax\log n)})$ against any oblivious
    adversarial sequence of length $T$ under full-information feedback.
    The rate matches Theorem~5.1 of \citet{balcan2015} with $k$ replaced
    by $\Kmax$; for constant $\Kmax$ it is $\tilde O(n\sqrt T)$.
  \item \textbf{Regime analysis (\Cref{cor:regime,cor:adaptive}).}
    We characterize the parameter regime in which the bound is meaningful
    ($\Kmax\cdot(n^2+\Kmax\log n)=o(T)$), and we prove an
    instance-adaptive refinement in which the leading factor depends on
    the number $K'$ of types \emph{actually} observed rather than on
    $\Kmax$.
  \item \textbf{Experiments (\Cref{sec:exp}).}
    We implement \textsc{Grow-Catalogue} and two reference baselines, and
    evaluate them on synthetic SSG instances. The empirical regret scales
    as predicted by the theorem, staying comfortably below the analytic
    $C\sqrt{T}$ envelope.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Classical SSGs and attacker uncertainty.}
The optimal commitment for a known attacker can be computed by an LP
\citep{conitzer2006}. Uncertainty about the attacker has been handled by
\emph{robust optimization}, which plans against worst-case payoffs
\citep{pita2010}, and by \emph{Bayesian SSGs}, which require a prior over
attacker types \citep{kiekintveld2011,marecki2012}. Both approaches rest on
priors or constraints that are often hard to validate. \citet{letchford2009}
give a query-efficient learning algorithm for a \emph{single} fixed but
unknown type, and \citet{blum2014b} extend this to a setting that adapts
round-by-round to one fixed type.

\paragraph{Online learning against adversarial attacker sequences.}
\citet{balcan2015} pivot from the single-type paradigm by allowing the
sequence of attackers to be adversarially chosen from a known finite set
$K$. For full information they give a polynomial-weights algorithm with
regret $O(\sqrt{Tn^2 k\log(nk)})$ that partitions the coverage polytope
into best-response regions and runs experts over the
$\epsilon$-approximate extreme points; for bandit feedback they use a
barycentric spanner construction to give an $O(T^{2/3})$ bound.
Crucially, they also prove a linear lower bound when $|K|$ is unrestricted,
which forces any non-trivial extension to restrict the attacker type set
somehow. We take the weakest restriction: a known cardinality bound
$\Kmax$.

\paragraph{Online learning with changing expert sets.}
The sub-routine inside \textsc{Grow-Catalogue} is an anytime variant of
Polynomial Weights / Hedge \citep{littlestone1994,freund1997,cesa2006}. We
restart this sub-routine each time the catalogue grows; simpler algorithms
for tracking a growing set of experts exist~\citep{mourtada2017}, and for
tracking a shrinking set \citep{gaillard2019}, but both would only shave
lower-order logarithmic factors.

\paragraph{Other recent directions.}
\citet{haghtalab2022} analyze no-regret learning against strategic
(non-myopic) attackers in a single-type query model. \citet{harris2024side}
extend to a contextual Stackelberg setting in which both players observe
side information. Neither addresses the \emph{unknown $K$ with bounded
cardinality} question that we pose here.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

We adopt the repeated-SSG notation of \citet[Sec.~2]{balcan2015}; our
notation is compatible with theirs.

\subsection{Game primitives}

The game has a finite horizon $T\in\NN$ and $n$ targets
$N=\{1,\dots,n\}$. The defender has $R$ resources and a collection
$\mathcal{D}\subseteq 2^N$ of \emph{schedules}, where each schedule is a
subset of targets that a single resource can defend simultaneously. An
\emph{assignment function} $A:R\to 2^{\mathcal{D}}$ records the schedules
that each resource may be assigned to. A \emph{pure strategy} is any valid
resource-to-schedule assignment; let $m$ denote the number of pure
strategies. A \emph{mixed strategy} is a distribution $\svec\in\Delta_m$
over pure strategies, and its induced \emph{coverage probability vector}
is $\pvec = M\svec\in [0,1]^n$, where $M\in\{0,1\}^{n\times m}$ has
$M_{ij}=1$ iff the $j$-th pure strategy covers target $i$. Write $\cP$ for
the set of valid coverage probability vectors;  Every coverage vector $\pvec$ is realizable by
some mixed strategy, and conversely every mixed strategy induces a unique
$\pvec\in\cP$.

\paragraph{Defender payoffs.}
For each target $i$, the defender has a payoff
$u_d^c(i)\in[-1,1]$ if $i$ is attacked \emph{and} covered, and a payoff
$u_d^u(i)\in[-1,1]$ if $i$ is attacked \emph{and uncovered}. The
defender's expected utility when target $i$ is attacked under coverage
$\pvec$ is therefore
\begin{equation}
U_d(i,\pvec)\;=\;u_d^c(i)\,p_i \;+\; u_d^u(i)\,(1-p_i),
\label{eq:Ud}
\end{equation}
which is \emph{linear} in $\pvec$ for each fixed target $i$.

\subsection{Attacker types and best response}

An attacker \emph{type} $\alpha$ is a pair
$(u_\alpha^c,u_\alpha^u)\in[-1,1]^n\times[-1,1]^n$ of per-target utilities
following the same ``covered/uncovered'' convention as the defender. Its
expected utility for attacking target $i$ under coverage $\pvec$ is
$
U_\alpha(i,\pvec)=u_\alpha^c(i)\,p_i + u_\alpha^u(i)\,(1-p_i).
$
Observing $\pvec$, type $\alpha$ \emph{best-responds}:
\begin{equation}
b_\alpha(\pvec)\;=\;\argmax_{i\in N}\,U_\alpha(i,\pvec),
\label{eq:br}
\end{equation}
with ties broken arbitrarily but consistently. Note that $b_\alpha$ is
piecewise constant in $\pvec$, which makes the defender's realized payoff
$U_d(b_\alpha(\pvec),\pvec)$ a discontinuous function of $\pvec$. This
non-continuity is the central technical difficulty that distinguishes SSGs
from vanilla online linear optimization, and it is what drives the
polytope-based machinery of \citet{balcan2015}.

\subsection{Repeated game and regret}

There is a (possibly unknown) set $K$ of attacker types. An
\emph{oblivious} adversary chooses a sequence
$\avec=a_1,\dots,a_T\in K^T$ \emph{before} the game starts; the sequence
is fixed but unknown to the defender. At each round
$t\in\{1,\dots,T\}$ the defender commits $\pvec_t\in\cP$, attacker
$a_t$ plays $b_{a_t}(\pvec_t)$, and the defender receives the random
payoff $U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)$. We assume \emph{full-information
feedback}: at the end of round $t$ the defender observes the identity of
$a_t$, and the first time any type $\alpha$ is observed, its utility
functions are revealed as well.

The defender's benchmark is the best fixed coverage vector in hindsight:
\begin{equation}
\pvec^{*}\;=\;\argmax_{\pvec\in\cP}\;\sum_{t=1}^T U_d\bigl(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec\bigr).
\label{eq:pstar}
\end{equation}
The expected regret of an online algorithm that plays $\pvec_t$ at round
$t$ is
\begin{equation}
\Regret(T)\;=\;\sum_{t=1}^T\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})\;-\;\EE\!\bigg[\sum_{t=1}^T\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)\bigg],
\label{eq:regret}
\end{equation}
where the expectation is over the algorithm's internal randomization.

\subsection{Preliminaries: polytope partition and $\cE(C;\epsilon)$}
\label{sec:polytopes}

The following definitions, taken from \citet[Sec.~4]{balcan2015}, are
essential. For any catalogue $C\subseteq K$, a type $\alpha\in C$, and a
target $i\in N$, the region in which $\alpha$'s best response is $i$ is
\begin{equation}
\cP^\alpha_i(C)\;=\;\{\pvec\in\cP\,:\,b_\alpha(\pvec)=i\},
\label{eq:Pia}
\end{equation}
which is the intersection of $\cP$ with the $n-1$ linear inequalities
$U_\alpha(i,\pvec)\ge U_\alpha(j,\pvec)$ for $j\ne i$; hence a convex
polytope. For a \emph{response profile}
$\sigma:C\to N$ we write
\begin{equation}
\cP_\sigma(C)\;=\;\bigcap_{\alpha\in C}\cP^\alpha_{\sigma(\alpha)}(C).
\label{eq:Psigma}
\end{equation}
The non-empty sets $\cP_\sigma(C)$ form a partition of $\cP$ into convex
regions in which the best response of every type in $C$ is held fixed;
within any such region, the defender's payoff under a fixed attacker type
is a linear function of $\pvec$.

We will need three results from \citet{balcan2015}, restated below in our
notation and cited explicitly at every use. \Cref{lem:43,lem:44} describe
the approximate extreme-point set; \Cref{prop:hedge} is the standard
Polynomial-Weights online learning guarantee, adapted to the present loss
range.

\begin{lemma}[{\citealp[Lemma 4.3]{balcan2015}}]
\label{lem:43}
For every $\epsilon>0$ and every catalogue $C\subseteq K$, there is a
finite set $\cE(C;\epsilon)\subseteq \cP$ of points, called the
$\epsilon$-approximate extreme points of the partition, such that for any
sequence $\avec'=a'_1,\dots,a'_{T'}$ with $a'_t\in C$ for all $t$,
\[
\max_{\pvec\in\cE(C;\epsilon)}\!\sum_{t=1}^{T'}\!U_d(b_{a'_t}(\pvec),\pvec)\,\ge\,\max_{\pvec\in\cP}\!\sum_{t=1}^{T'}\!U_d(b_{a'_t}(\pvec),\pvec)-2\epsilon\,T'.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[{\citealp[Lemma 4.4]{balcan2015}}]
\label{lem:44}
The set $\cE(C;\epsilon)$ of \Cref{lem:43} can be chosen so that
\(|\cE(C;\epsilon)|\in O\!\bigl((2^n+|C|\,n^2)^n\cdot n^{|C|}\bigr).\)
\end{lemma}

\begin{proposition}[{\citealp[Proposition 1]{balcan2015}}]
\label{prop:hedge}
For any fixed expert set $\cM$ and any sequence of losses in
$[-\kappa,\kappa]$, there is an online learning algorithm (an anytime
variant of Polynomial Weights / Hedge; see \citealp[Sec.~2]{cesa2006}) whose
expected regret after $T'$ rounds is at most
$O\bigl(\sqrt{T'\,\kappa\,\log|\cM|}\bigr)$, without requiring $T'$ to be
known in advance.
\end{proposition}

\section{The Unknown-$K$ Setting}
\label{sec:unknownK}

We now state the problem that our algorithm and theorem address.

\begin{problem}[Unknown-$K$ repeated SSG]\label{prob:unknownK}
A repeated SSG as in \Cref{sec:model} is played, with two modifications.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=0.05em,topsep=0.2em]
  \item The set $K$ of attacker types is not revealed to the defender at
  the start; however, the defender is given a constant $\Kmax\ge 1$ with
  the promise $|K|\le\Kmax$.
  \item Feedback is full information: after round $t$ the defender
  observes $a_t$, and the first time any type $\alpha$ appears, its
  utility vectors $(u_\alpha^c,u_\alpha^u)$ are revealed.
\end{enumerate}
The benchmark $\pvec^{*}$ and regret $\Regret(T)$ are as in
\Cref{eq:pstar,eq:regret}.
\end{problem}

\begin{remark}[Necessity of the bound $\Kmax$]\label{rem:necessity}
\citet[Theorem 7.1]{balcan2015} exhibit, for every $T$, a game with
$|K|<2^{T+1}$ on which every online algorithm has expected regret at
least $T/2$. Hence some finite bound on $|K|$ is \emph{necessary} for
sublinear regret; \Cref{prob:unknownK} takes the weakest such bound,
namely a known constant $\Kmax$.
\end{remark}

\section{Algorithm and Main Result}
\label{sec:alg}

\subsection{The \textsc{Grow-Catalogue} algorithm}

We describe \textsc{Grow-Catalogue} at a high level and then give the
pseudocode in \Cref{alg:gc}. At any round $t$ the defender holds a
catalogue $C_t\subseteq K$ of all types seen so far. Conceptually, the
defender runs a small instance of the \emph{known-$K$} algorithm of
\citet[Sec.~5]{balcan2015} on the catalogue $C_t$, pretending that $C_t$
\emph{is} the full attacker set. Concretely:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em,itemsep=0.05em,topsep=0.2em]
  \item The algorithm maintains the $\epsilon$-approximate extreme-point
  set $\cE_t=\cE(C_t;\epsilon)$ of \Cref{lem:43}. We set
  $\epsilon=1/\sqrt T$; this balances the $2\epsilon T$ approximation term
  in \Cref{lem:43} against the $\sqrt{T\log\Nmax}$ regret term of
  \Cref{prop:hedge}.
  \item An anytime Polynomial-Weights sub-routine of
  \Cref{prop:hedge} runs over $\cE_t$, updating via a time-varying
  learning rate $\eta_{t_{\text{loc}}}=\sqrt{\log|\cE_t|/t_{\text{loc}}}$
  where $t_{\text{loc}}$ is a local counter reset at every catalogue
  change. This is the standard anytime schedule; see \citet[Sec.~2]{cesa2006}.
  \item When a new attacker type $\alpha$ appears, the catalogue grows to
  $C_t\cup\{\alpha\}$, the approximate extreme-point set is recomputed,
  and the sub-routine is restarted on the enlarged expert set. The single
  round of first contact with $\alpha$ contributes a bounded additive
  amount to the regret that we will absorb.
\end{itemize}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Grow-Catalogue} (full information).}
\label{alg:gc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\small
\Require horizon $T$, bound $\Kmax$, game primitives, default $\pvec_{\text{def}}\in\cP$
\State $C\gets\emptyset$;~$\cE\gets\emptyset$;~$\epsilon\gets 1/\sqrt T$;~$t_{\text{loc}}\gets 0$
\For{$t=1,\dots,T$}
  \If{$\cE=\emptyset$} \State play $\pvec_t\gets\pvec_{\text{def}}$
  \Else \State sample $\pvec_t\sim q_t$, where $q_t$ is the current PW distribution over $\cE$
  \EndIf
  \State observe $a_t$
  \If{$a_t\notin C$} \Comment{discovery round: new attacker type}
    \State $C\gets C\cup\{a_t\}$; record $(u_{a_t}^c,u_{a_t}^u)$
    \State $\cE\gets\cE(C;\epsilon)$ \Comment{recompute extreme points}
    \State reset PW on $\cE$; $t_{\text{loc}}\gets 0$
  \Else \Comment{routine round}
    \State $t_{\text{loc}}\gets t_{\text{loc}}+1$
    \State compute $\ell_t(\pvec)\!=\!-U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)$ for $\pvec\in\cE$
    \State $\eta\gets\sqrt{\log|\cE|/t_{\text{loc}}}$
    \State $w_\pvec\gets w_\pvec\cdot e^{-\eta\,\ell_t(\pvec)}$ for all $\pvec\in\cE$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{Main Result}
\label{sec:mainresult}

\begin{theorem}[Main]
\label{thm:main}
Let the defender play \Cref{alg:gc} in the unknown-$K$ full-information
setting of \Cref{prob:unknownK}. For any oblivious adversarial sequence
$\avec\in K^T$ with $|K|\le\Kmax$, the expected regret satisfies
\begin{equation}
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;\le\;O\!\Bigl(\sqrt{T\cdot\Kmax\cdot(n^2+\Kmax\log n)}\Bigr).
\label{eq:main}
\end{equation}
In particular, if $\Kmax=O(1)$ then $\EE[\Regret(T)]=\tilde O(n\sqrt T)$,
matching the known-$K$ bound of \citet[Theorem 5.1]{balcan2015} up to
logarithmic factors.
\end{theorem}

We prove \Cref{thm:main} in detail in \Cref{sec:proof}. Two useful
corollaries of the proof (the regime of applicability and an
instance-adaptive refinement) are stated after the proof in
\Cref{sec:consequences}.

% ==========================================================================
\section{Proof of \Cref{thm:main}}
\label{sec:proof}

The proof closely tracks the structure of the known-$K$ proof of \citet[Sec.~5]{balcan2015}
but has two non-trivial deviations: (i) the expert set is no longer static
but grows over time, and (ii) the discovery rounds introduce an extra
bounded additive term. We account for both the deviations in our proof.

\begin{proof}[Proof:- ]
Fix an oblivious adversarial sequence $\avec=a_1,\dots,a_T\in K^T$ with
$|K|\le\Kmax$. Let $K'=|\{a_1,\dots,a_T\}|\le\Kmax$ denote the number of
distinct types that actually appear in $\avec$. The proof proceeds in ten
steps.

\paragraph{Step 1 (Epoch decomposition).}
Let $\tau_1<\tau_2<\cdots<\tau_{K'}$ be the round indices at which each
new attacker type is first observed, i.e., for each
$i\in\{1,\dots,K'\}$, round $\tau_i$ is the smallest $t$ such that
$a_t\notin\{a_1,\dots,a_{t-1}\}$. We define the \emph{epochs}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Ep}_i\;=\;\{\tau_i+1,\tau_i+2,\dots,\tau_{i+1}-1\},\quad
i=1,\dots,K',
\label{eq:epoch-def}
\end{equation}
with the convention $\tau_{K'+1}\coloneqq T+1$, and write
$L_i=|\mathrm{Ep}_i|$. Let
$\mathcal{D}=\{\tau_1,\dots,\tau_{K'}\}$ be the set of \emph{discovery
rounds}. By construction,
\[
[1,T]\;=\;\mathcal{D}\;\sqcup\;\bigcup_{i=1}^{K'}\mathrm{Ep}_i,
\]
and
$|\mathcal{D}|+\sum_{i=1}^{K'}L_i=T$ with $|\mathcal{D}|=K'\le\Kmax$. The
epoch decomposition is the critical structural property of the proof: it
re-interprets the online problem with a growing expert set as a finite
sequence of online problems with \emph{fixed} expert sets, so that the
known-$K$ analysis of \citet{balcan2015} can be invoked inside each
epoch.

\paragraph{Step 2 (Catalogue is constant inside an epoch).}
Immediately after round $\tau_i$, the catalogue maintained by
\Cref{alg:gc} is $C_i=\{a_{\tau_1},\dots,a_{\tau_i}\}$, and it is not
updated until the next discovery round $\tau_{i+1}$. Hence $C_t=C_i$ for
every $t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i$. Moreover, by the defining property of
$\tau_{i+1}$, no new type appears in $\mathrm{Ep}_i$, so
\begin{equation}
a_t\in C_i\quad\text{for every }t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i.
\label{eq:epoch-types}
\end{equation}
Denote by $\cE_i\coloneqq\cE(C_i;\epsilon)$ the extreme-point set
used by the PW sub-routine during $\mathrm{Ep}_i$; \Cref{eq:epoch-types}
is exactly the hypothesis needed to apply \Cref{lem:43} to $C_i$ and the
sub-sequence inside $\mathrm{Ep}_i$.

\paragraph{Step 3 (Within-epoch regret).}
During $\mathrm{Ep}_i$ the algorithm runs a fresh instance of the anytime
Polynomial-Weights sub-routine on the fixed expert set $\cE_i$ for $L_i$
rounds. The losses are $\ell_t(\pvec)=-U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)\in[-1,1]$,
so the range parameter is $\kappa=1$. \Cref{prop:hedge} therefore
yields an absolute constant $c_1>0$ such that
\begin{align}
&\max_{\pvec\in\cE_i}\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)\notag\\
&\quad-\EE\!\left[\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)\right]\;\le\;c_1\sqrt{L_i\log|\cE_i|}.
\label{eq:hedge-epoch}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Step 4 (Within-epoch approximation via \Cref{lem:43}).}
We now wish to compare the max over $\cE_i$ during $\mathrm{Ep}_i$ with
the \emph{global} benchmark $\pvec^{*}$ (the best fixed strategy over the
full horizon $[1,T]$). \Cref{lem:43}, however, is a statement of the form
``$\max_{\pvec\in\cE(C;\epsilon)}(\cdot)\ge\max_{\pvec\in\cP}(\cdot)-2\epsilon T'$''
and, strictly speaking, does \emph{not} directly involve $\pvec^{*}$: it
compares the max over the finite set $\cE(C;\epsilon)$ against the
unrestricted max over $\cP$, both on the \emph{same} attacker sequence.
We therefore proceed in two sub-steps and make the passage to $\pvec^{*}$
explicit.

\emph{Sub-step 4a (apply \Cref{lem:43} to the epoch sub-sequence).}
By \Cref{eq:epoch-types}, the sub-sequence $(a_t)_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}$
has length $L_i$ and uses only types in $C_i$, which is exactly the
hypothesis of \Cref{lem:43}. Applying the lemma with catalogue $C_i$ and
sequence length $T'=L_i$ gives
\begin{align}
&\max_{\pvec\in\cE_i}\!\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)\notag\\
&\quad\ge\;\underbrace{\max_{\pvec\in\cP}\!\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)}_{\eqqcolon\;\mathrm{OPT}_i}-2\epsilon L_i.
\label{eq:lem43-epoch}
\end{align}
Here $\mathrm{OPT}_i$ is the best cumulative utility achievable on
$\mathrm{Ep}_i$ by \emph{any} coverage vector in $\cP$; crucially, this
is the epoch-$i$ optimum, which in general \emph{differs} from the value
of $\pvec^{*}$. This is the subtle point that distinguishes
our setting from the known-$K$ proof of \citet[Sec.~5]{balcan2015}: in their
analysis, \Cref{lem:43} is applied once on the full horizon, so
$\mathrm{OPT}=\sum_{t=1}^T U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})$ by
definition of $\pvec^{*}$ and no separate passage is needed. In our
per-epoch application, $\mathrm{OPT}_i$ is an epoch-local quantity,
potentially larger than $\pvec^{*}$'s value on the epoch.

\emph{Sub-step 4b (pass from $\mathrm{OPT}_i$ to $\pvec^{*}$).}
Since $\pvec^{*}\in\cP$, the coverage vector $\pvec^{*}$ is a \emph{feasible}
competitor in the maximization defining $\mathrm{OPT}_i$. Therefore
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{OPT}_i\;\ge\;\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*}).
\label{eq:opt-vs-pstar}
\end{equation}
Inequality \Cref{eq:opt-vs-pstar} is \emph{not} a consequence of
\Cref{lem:43} but of the trivial fact that the global benchmark
$\pvec^{*}$ is an element of the set over which the epoch-local optimum
is taken. Chaining \Cref{eq:lem43-epoch} with \Cref{eq:opt-vs-pstar},
\begin{align}
&\max_{\pvec\in\cE_i}\!\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)\notag\\
&\quad\ge\;\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})-2\epsilon L_i.
\label{eq:lem43-pstar}
\end{align}


\paragraph{Step 5 (Per-epoch combined bound).}
Chaining \Cref{eq:hedge-epoch,eq:lem43-pstar}, for every epoch
$i\in\{1,\dots,K'\}$ we obtain
\begin{align}
&\EE\!\left[\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)\right]\notag\\
&\;\ge\;\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Ep}_i}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})-2\epsilon L_i-c_1\sqrt{L_i\log|\cE_i|}.\label{eq:combined-epoch}
\end{align}
Equation \Cref{eq:combined-epoch} is the per-epoch regret bound
\emph{against the global benchmark} $\pvec^{*}$, and it is the building
block for the rest of the proof. Note that despite the fact that
$\pvec^{*}$ is the benchmark over all $T$ rounds (not just epoch $i$),
we are able to bound our per-epoch regret against $\pvec^{*}$ because
$\pvec^{*}\in\cP$ and we applied \Cref{lem:43} on the sub-sequence.

\paragraph{Step 6 (Discovery rounds).}
For each discovery round $t\in\mathcal{D}$, the algorithm either plays
$\pvec_{\text{def}}$ (if $t=\tau_1$) or samples $\pvec_t$ from the PW
distribution inherited from $\mathrm{Ep}_{i-1}$ (if $t=\tau_i$ for
$i\ge 2$). In both cases the sampled $\pvec_t$ is not tuned to the
newly-appearing type $a_t=a_{\tau_i}$. However, because the payoff
$U_d$ is bounded in $[-1,1]$, for every $\pvec\in\cP$ and every type
$\alpha$, $U_d(b_\alpha(\pvec),\pvec)\in[-1,1]$, so
\begin{equation}
\bigl|U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})-U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)\bigr|\;\le\;2.
\label{eq:disc-bound}
\end{equation}
Summing \Cref{eq:disc-bound} over the at most $K'\le\Kmax$ discovery rounds
yields
\begin{equation}
\sum_{t\in\mathcal{D}}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})-\EE\!\left[\sum_{t\in\mathcal{D}}\!U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)\right]\le 2\Kmax.
\label{eq:disc-sum}
\end{equation}
This is the only source of additive dependence on $\Kmax$ that does not
come from the main $\sqrt{T\Kmax}$ term. It is the ``price of not knowing
$K$'' at a discovery round.

\paragraph{Step 7 (Uniform bound on $|\cE_i|$).}
By \Cref{lem:44} applied with $|C_i|\le\Kmax$,
\[
|\cE_i|\;\le\;c_2\,(2^n+\Kmax n^2)^n\cdot n^{\Kmax}\;\eqqcolon\;\Nmax,
\]
for an absolute constant $c_2$. Taking logarithms,
\begin{align}
\log\Nmax &= n\log\!\bigl(2^n+\Kmax n^2\bigr)+\Kmax\log n+O(1)\notag\\
&\le n^2+n\log\!\bigl(1+\tfrac{\Kmax n^2}{2^{n-1}}\bigr)+\Kmax\log n\notag\\
&= O\bigl(n^2+\Kmax\log n\bigr),
\label{eq:logN}
\end{align}
where the middle step uses $\log(2^n+\Kmax n^2)\le n+\log(1+\Kmax n^2/2^{n-1})$
and $\log(1+x)\le x$ for $x>0$. Crucially, \Cref{eq:logN} is uniform in
$i$: $\log|\cE_i|\le\log\Nmax$ for every epoch. This lets us pull the
$\log|\cE_i|$ factor in Step~5 outside the sum over epochs.

\paragraph{Step 8 (Summing across epochs).}
Sum \Cref{eq:combined-epoch} over $i=1,\dots,K'$ and add
\Cref{eq:disc-sum}. The algorithm's total expected utility is then at
least the benchmark's total utility, minus the discovery term $2\Kmax$,
minus $2\epsilon T$ from the telescoping Lemma-4.3 approximation errors
(using $\sum_i L_i\le T$), minus the sum of the per-epoch PW regret
terms. Rearranging,
\begin{align}
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;\le\;2\Kmax+2\epsilon T+c_1\sqrt{\log\Nmax}\!\sum_{i=1}^{K'}\!\sqrt{L_i}.
\label{eq:after-sum}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Step 9 (Cauchy--Schwarz).}
The last term involves the sum of $K'$ square roots. By the Cauchy--Schwarz
inequality applied to $(1,\dots,1)\in\RR^{K'}$ and
$(\sqrt{L_1},\dots,\sqrt{L_{K'}})$,
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^{K'}\!\sqrt{L_i}\;\le\;\sqrt{K'\cdot\!\sum_{i=1}^{K'}\!L_i}\;\le\;\sqrt{\Kmax\,T}.
\label{eq:CS}
\end{equation}
The first inequality is tight when all epochs have equal length, a
scenario that corresponds to an adversary who reveals types evenly across
the horizon. This is where the leading multiplicative $\sqrt{\Kmax}$
factor in the bound is generated. An instance-adaptive version uses $K'$
in place of $\Kmax$, which is what \Cref{cor:adaptive} records.

\paragraph{Step 10 (Final substitution).}
Plugging \Cref{eq:logN,eq:CS} into \Cref{eq:after-sum} and choosing
$\epsilon=1/\sqrt T$ gives
\begin{align*}
\EE[\Regret(T)]&\le 2\Kmax+2\sqrt T+c_1\sqrt{\Kmax T\,\log\Nmax}\\
&\le 2\Kmax+2\sqrt T\\
&\quad+c_3\sqrt{T\Kmax(n^2+\Kmax\log n)}
\end{align*}
for an absolute constant $c_3$. Whenever $T\ge\Kmax$ (the only regime in
which the statement of \Cref{thm:main} is non-trivial) the leading
$\sqrt{T\Kmax(n^2+\Kmax\log n)}$ term dominates the additive
$2\Kmax+2\sqrt T$, so
\[
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;=\;O\!\Bigl(\sqrt{T\cdot\Kmax\cdot(n^2+\Kmax\log n)}\Bigr),
\]
which is exactly the bound of \Cref{thm:main}. This completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Consequences of the proof}
\label{sec:consequences}

Two corollaries follow directly from the proof of \Cref{thm:main} above.
The first characterizes the regime in which the bound is non-trivial,
the second gives an instance-adaptive refinement.

\begin{corollary}[Regime of applicability]
\label{cor:regime}
The bound of \Cref{thm:main} is sublinear in $T$ if and only if
$\Kmax\cdot(n^2+\Kmax\log n)=o(T)$; equivalently,
$\Kmax=o\!\bigl(\min\{T/n^2,\sqrt{T/\log n}\}\bigr)$.
In particular, for $\Kmax=O(1)$ the rate is $\tilde O(n\sqrt T)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Set the right-hand side of \Cref{eq:main} equal to $T$ and solve for
$\Kmax$. The condition $\Kmax\cdot(n^2+\Kmax\log n)=o(T)$ is exactly
when the bound of \Cref{thm:main} is $o(T)$; the equivalent form follows
by separately bounding $\Kmax\cdot n^2\le T/\omega(1)$ and
$\Kmax^2\log n\le T/\omega(1)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Instance-adaptive bound]
\label{cor:adaptive}
Let $K'=|\{a_1,\dots,a_T\}|\le\Kmax$ be the number of \emph{distinct}
attacker types actually observed in the realized sequence. Then
\Cref{alg:gc} achieves
\[
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;\le\;O\!\Bigl(\sqrt{T\cdot K'\cdot(n^2+K'\log n)}\Bigr).
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The Cauchy--Schwarz step \Cref{eq:CS} of the proof of \Cref{thm:main}
gives $\sum_{i=1}^{K'}\sqrt{L_i}\le\sqrt{K'\cdot T}$; plugging this
sharper bound into \Cref{eq:after-sum} and redoing Step~10 with $K'$ in
place of $\Kmax$ gives the stated bound.\end{proof}

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Setup}

We implement \Cref{alg:gc} in Python 
, along
with two reference algorithms: the known-$K$ oracle of
\citet[Sec.~5]{balcan2015}, which receives the full set $K$ at $t=1$; and a
\emph{uniform} baseline that plays $\pvec_{\text{def}}=(R/n,\dots,R/n)$
every round. Unless stated otherwise, we use $n=3$ targets, $R=1$
resource, and attacker utilities drawn i.i.d.\ from $\mathrm{Unif}[-1,1]$
with $u_\alpha^u(i)>0>u_\alpha^c(i)$ to ensure covering is costly for the
attacker. The extreme-point set $\cE(C;\epsilon)$ is computed by direct
enumeration of intersections of $(n-1)$-subsets of the defining
hyperplanes, which is tractable at $n=3$.

We use a \emph{staggered-uniform} adversary: the horizon $[0,T)$ is
partitioned into $\Kmax$ phases of length $T/\Kmax$ each, and in phase
$i$ the attacker is drawn uniformly at random from the first $i$ types
$\{\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_i\}$. Thus $\alpha_1$ is present in every
phase, $\alpha_2$ from phase 2 onward, and so on. This design produces a
clean sequence of discovery rounds at $t=0,T/\Kmax,\dots,(\Kmax-1)T/\Kmax$,
while still mixing old and new types after each reveal so that the
benchmark $\pvec^{*}$ is a genuine compromise rather than a specialist
strategy.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conc}

We studied the repeated Stackelberg security game in a new, open regime
in which the set of attacker types is unknown to the defender a priori
but known to have bounded cardinality. We presented \textsc{Grow-Catalogue},
a simple online algorithm that incrementally discovers attacker types and
re-partitions the coverage polytope accordingly, and we gave a full proof
that its expected regret under full-information feedback is at most
$O\!\bigl(\sqrt{T\cdot\Kmax\cdot(n^2+\Kmax\log n)}\bigr)$. This is the
same functional form as the known-$K$ bound of \citet[Theorem 5.1]{balcan2015},
with the known cardinality $k$ replaced by our known upper bound $\Kmax$.
The bound is sublinear whenever $\Kmax\cdot(n^2+\Kmax\log n)=o(T)$, and
the matching $\Omega(T)$ lower bound of \citet[Theorem 7.1]{balcan2015}
rules out further improvements on the unbounded side. Our experiments
confirm the analytical scaling: the empirical regret stays below the
$C\sqrt T$ envelope and tracks the epoch structure of the proof.

\subsection{Erratum: Step 7 of the proof of Theorem 6}
\label{sec:erratum}

Step~7 of \Cref{sec:proof} bounds $\log\Nmax$ by the chain
\begin{align*}
\log\Nmax &= n\log(2^n+\Kmax n^2)+\Kmax\log n+O(1)\\
&\le n^2+n\log\bigl(1+\tfrac{\Kmax n^2}{2^{n-1}}\bigr)+\Kmax\log n,
\end{align*}
and then asserts that the right-hand side is $O(n^2+\Kmax\log n)$, ``using
$\log(1+x)\le x$ for $x>0$''. That last step is not valid as written, for
two separate reasons.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.15em,topsep=0.2em]
\item The inequality $\log(1+x)\le x$ is a statement about the
\emph{natural} logarithm. The same display uses $\log 2^n=n$, i.e.\ base
$2$, and $\log_2(1+x)>x$ for every $x\in(0,1)$.
\item Even granting the inequality, it is applied exactly where it is
least informative. It yields the bound $\Kmax n^3/2^{n-1}$ for a quantity
whose true size is logarithmic in $\Kmax$. For $n=2$ and $\Kmax=10^6$, for
instance, $n\log(2^n+\Kmax n^2)=43.9$ whereas the substitute expression
$n^2+\Kmax n^3/2^{n-1}$ equals $4.0\times 10^{6}$ --- an overestimate by a
factor of about $9\times 10^{4}$. A first-order expansion of the logarithm
is simply the wrong tool for a term in which the argument of the logarithm
is unbounded.
\end{enumerate}

\Cref{lem:logN-fixed} restores the conclusion by the route actually taken
by \citet[proof of Theorem 5.1]{balcan2015}, namely bounding a sum inside a
logarithm by its larger summand rather than expanding it.

\begin{lemma}[Uniform expert-set bound; corrected Step 7]
\label{lem:logN-fixed}
Let $n\ge 2$ and $\Kmax\ge 2$. For every catalogue $C\subseteq K$ with
$|C|\le\Kmax$ and every $\epsilon>0$,
\begin{align*}
\log|\cE(C;\epsilon)|\;\le\;\log\Nmax
&\;\le\;5n^2+2\Kmax\log n+O(1)\\
&\;=\;O\bigl(n^2+\Kmax\log n\bigr),
\end{align*}
where $\Nmax=c_2(2^n+\Kmax n^2)^n\,n^{\Kmax}$ is as in Step~7.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The first inequality is \Cref{lem:44} together with the monotonicity of
the bound in $|C|$. Taking logarithms,
\begin{equation}
\log\Nmax=n\log\bigl(2^n+\Kmax n^2\bigr)+\Kmax\log n+O(1).
\label{eq:logN-start}
\end{equation}
For any $a,b>0$ we have $a+b\le 2\max\{a,b\}$ and hence
$\log(a+b)\le 1+\max\{\log a,\log b\}$. Applying this with $a=2^n$ and
$b=\Kmax n^2$,
\begin{align}
\log\bigl(2^n+\Kmax n^2\bigr)
&\;\le\;1+\max\{\,n,\;\log\Kmax+2\log n\,\}\notag\\
&\;\le\;1+n+\log\Kmax+2\log n ,
\label{eq:maxtrick}
\end{align}
so that
$n\log(2^n+\Kmax n^2)\le n+n^2+n\log\Kmax+2n\log n$.
We claim that
\begin{equation}
n\log\Kmax\;\le\;n^2+\Kmax\log n .
\label{eq:absorb}
\end{equation}
Indeed, if $\Kmax\le 2^n$ then $n\log\Kmax\le n\cdot n=n^2$. If
$\Kmax>2^n$, consider $f(x)=x/\log x$, which is increasing on
$[e,\infty)$. For $n\ge 3$ we have $e\le n<2^n<\Kmax$, so
$f(\Kmax)\ge f(n)$, i.e.\ $n\log\Kmax\le\Kmax\log n$; for $n=2$ the
hypothesis $\Kmax>2^n=4$ gives $\Kmax\ge 5$ and
$f(\Kmax)\ge f(5)=5/\log 5>2=f(2)$, so the same conclusion holds. This
proves \Cref{eq:absorb}. Using $2n\log n\le 2n^2$ and $n\le n^2$ and
substituting into \Cref{eq:logN-start},
\[
\log\Nmax\;\le\;5n^2+2\Kmax\log n+O(1),
\]
which is the claim: the five $n^2$ terms come from $n$, $n^2$, the $n^2$
of \Cref{eq:absorb} and $2n\log n\le 2n^2$, and the two $\Kmax\log n$
terms from \Cref{eq:absorb} and \Cref{eq:logN-start}.
\end{proof}

\Cref{lem:logN-fixed} delivers exactly \Cref{eq:logN}, so Steps~8--10 of
\Cref{sec:proof}, \Cref{thm:main}, \Cref{cor:regime} and
\Cref{cor:adaptive} all stand unchanged. Only the justification inside
Step~7 needs replacing.

\begin{remark}[On the comparison with \citeauthor{balcan2015}]
\label{rem:comparison}
The corrected computation also makes visible a second inaccuracy, in the
\emph{prose} around \Cref{thm:main}: the abstract, \Cref{thm:main} and
\Cref{sec:conc} describe our rate as matching
\citet[Theorem 5.1]{balcan2015} ``with $k$ replaced by $\Kmax$''. That is
not right. The bound displayed in \citet[Theorem 5.1]{balcan2015},
$O(\sqrt{Tn^2k\log nk})$, is itself a loosening: what their proof
establishes is $O(\sqrt{T\log|\cE|})$, and by exactly the computation of
\Cref{lem:logN-fixed} with $\Kmax$ replaced by $k$ this equals
\[
O\Bigl(\sqrt{T\,(n^2+k\log n)}\Bigr)
\qquad\text{(sharp known-$K$ rate).}
\]
Written against this sharp form, \Cref{thm:main} is
$O(\sqrt{T\,\Kmax\,\Lam})$ versus $O(\sqrt{T\,\Lam})$ with $k=\Kmax$: a
multiplicative $\sqrt{\Kmax}$ worse, generated entirely by the
Cauchy--Schwarz step (Step~9) over the $K'\le\Kmax$ epochs. That factor is
the price of not knowing $K$, and it should be stated as such rather than
described as a match. The claim ``for $\Kmax=O(1)$ the rate is
$\tilde O(n\sqrt T)$'' is correct, since then $\Kmax$ is absorbed into the
constant.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Direction 1: periodic type revelation}
\label{sec:periodic}

\Cref{prob:unknownK} couples two things that need not be coupled: the
moment a type \emph{acts} and the moment the defender \emph{learns what
that type wants}. In deployment the second is usually the output of an intelligence
process that reports on a schedule --- a weekly threat briefing, a monthly
poaching-patrol debrief --- rather than instantaneously. We therefore
study the model in which the catalogue is delivered in instalments of
period $P$.

\subsubsection{Model and algorithm}

\begin{problem}[Periodic revelation, $\mathrm{PR}$-$P$]
\label{prob:pr}
Fix a period $P\in\{1,\dots,T\}$ and partition the horizon into
$B=\lceil T/P\rceil$ blocks
$\cB_j=\{(j-1)P+1,\dots,\min(jP,T)\}$. A repeated SSG as in
\Cref{sec:model} is played with $|K|\le\Kmax$ and $K$ unknown, and with
the following feedback.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.05em,topsep=0.15em]
  \item After each round $t$ the defender observes an \emph{identifier}
  for $a_t$: enough to decide whether $a_t$ has been encountered before,
  but nothing about its payoffs.
  \item At each \emph{checkpoint} $t=jP$, $j=1,\dots,B$, the defender is
  given the utility vectors $(u^c_\alpha,u^u_\alpha)$ of every type that
  has appeared in rounds $1,\dots,jP$.
\end{enumerate}
Thus the defender's \emph{catalogue} at round $t$ is
$C_t=\{a_s: s\le P\lfloor (t-1)/P\rfloor\}$, which is constant on each
block. The benchmark $\pvec^{*}$ and $\Regret(T)$ are as in
\Cref{eq:pstar,eq:regret}.
\end{problem}

Taking $P=1$ recovers \Cref{prob:unknownK} exactly: the checkpoint at the
end of round $t$ discloses $a_t$'s dossier, so $C_t=\{a_1,\dots,a_{t-1}\}$,
which is the catalogue \Cref{alg:gc} holds when it plays $\pvec_t$.
Increasing $P$ batches the same information into fewer, larger deliveries.

Two features of $\mathrm{PR}$-$P$ drive the analysis.

\paragraph{Deterministic epoch boundaries.} The catalogue can change only
at a checkpoint, so the epoch structure of \Cref{sec:proof} is now
anchored to a grid known to the defender in advance. Write
$D_j=\{a_t:t\le jP\}$ for the types instantiated by the end of block $j$
(so $D_0=\emptyset$ and block $j$ is played with catalogue $D_{j-1}$), and
let
\[
G=\{\,j\in[B]\;:\;D_j\supsetneq D_{j-1}\,\},\qquad M\coloneqq |G| .
\]
Every $j\in G$ contributes at least one type never seen before, so
\begin{equation}
M\;\le\;\min\{K',\,B\}\;\le\;\min\{\Kmax,\,\lceil T/P\rceil\}.
\label{eq:Mbound}
\end{equation}
We write $\Mbar\coloneqq\min\{\Kmax,\lceil T/P\rceil\}$ for the right-hand
side of \Cref{eq:Mbound}, a quantity the defender knows in advance. The
catalogue changes only at the starts of the blocks $j+1$ with $j\in G$, so
the horizon splits into at most $M+1$ \emph{epochs} on which the catalogue
--- and hence the expert set --- is constant.

\paragraph{Blind rounds.} Call round $t$ \emph{blind} if $a_t\notin C_t$.
On a blind round the defender cannot evaluate
$\ell_t(\pvec)=-U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)$ for $\pvec\in\cE$, because it
does not hold $a_t$'s payoffs, and so cannot update its weights. This is
the one genuinely new cost in $\mathrm{PR}$-$P$; \Cref{lem:blind} bounds
how many such rounds there can be.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Periodic-Grow-Catalogue} ($\mathrm{PR}$-$P$).}
\label{alg:pgc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\small
\Require horizon $T$, period $P$, game primitives, default $\pvec_{\text{def}}\in\cP$
\State $C\gets\emptyset$;~$\cE\gets\emptyset$;~$\epsilon\gets 1/\sqrt T$;~$t_{\text{loc}}\gets 0$
\For{$t=1,\dots,T$}
  \If{$\cE=\emptyset$} \State play $\pvec_t\gets\pvec_{\text{def}}$
  \Else \State sample $\pvec_t\sim q_t$, the current PW distribution over $\cE$
  \EndIf
  \State observe the identifier of $a_t$
  \If{$a_t\in C$} \Comment{informed round}
    \State compute $\ell_t(\pvec)\!=\!-U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)$ for $\pvec\in\cE$
    \State $t_{\text{loc}}\gets t_{\text{loc}}+1$;~$\eta\gets\sqrt{\log|\cE|/t_{\text{loc}}}$
    \State $w_\pvec\gets w_\pvec\,e^{-\eta\,\ell_t(\pvec)}$ for all $\pvec\in\cE$
  \Else \Comment{blind round: freeze the weights}
  \EndIf
  \If{$t\equiv 0 \pmod P$} \Comment{checkpoint}
    \State receive the dossiers of all types seen so far; $C'\gets\{a_s:s\le t\}$
    \If{$C'\neq C$} \Comment{lazy restart}
      \State $C\gets C'$;~$\cE\gets\cE(C;\epsilon)$;~reset PW on $\cE$;~$t_{\text{loc}}\gets 0$
    \EndIf
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\Cref{alg:pgc} is \Cref{alg:gc} with the catalogue update moved onto the
checkpoint grid, and with one deliberate change: the Polynomial-Weights
sub-routine is restarted \emph{lazily}, only when the catalogue actually
grows, and not at every block boundary. \Cref{rem:naive} below shows how
much that matters.

\subsubsection{Regret analysis}

\begin{lemma}[Blind rounds]
\label{lem:blind}
Let $\bar S=\{t\in[T]:a_t\notin C_t\}$ be the set of blind rounds. Then
$|\bar S|\le\min\{M P,\,T\}$, and
\begin{multline*}
\sum_{t\in\bar S}U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})\\
-\EE\Bigl[\sum_{t\in\bar S}U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)\Bigr]
\;\le\;2\min\{MP,T\}.
\end{multline*}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $t\in\cB_j$. Then $C_t=D_{j-1}$, so $t$ is blind exactly when $a_t$
first appears during block $j$, which forces $D_j\supsetneq D_{j-1}$, i.e.\
$j\in G$. Hence $\bar S\subseteq\bigcup_{j\in G}\cB_j$ and
$|\bar S|\le|G|\cdot P=MP$; the bound $|\bar S|\le T$ is trivial. The
second claim follows because $U_d(i,\pvec)\in[-1,1]$ for every target and
every $\pvec\in\cP$, so each round contributes at most $2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Regret under periodic revelation]
\label{thm:pr}
Let the defender play \Cref{alg:pgc} in the setting of \Cref{prob:pr}. For
any oblivious adversarial sequence $\avec\in K^T$ with $|K|\le\Kmax$,
\begin{align}
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;\le\;2\sqrt T
&+c_1\sqrt{(M+1)\,T\,\log\Nmax}\notag\\
&+2\min\{MP,T\},
\label{eq:pr-exact}
\end{align}
with $M\le\Mbar=\min\{\Kmax,\lceil T/P\rceil\}$ as in \Cref{eq:Mbound}
and $c_1$ the constant of \Cref{prop:hedge}. Consequently, with $\Lam$ as
in \Cref{eq:Lambda},
\begin{equation}
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;=\;O\Bigl(\sqrt{T\,\Mbar\,\Lam}\;+\;\Mbar\,P\Bigr).
\label{eq:pr}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix the sequence $\avec$ and let $\Ep_0,\dots,\Ep_{M'}$, $M'\le M$, be the
epochs on which the catalogue is constant, with catalogues $C^{(i)}$,
expert sets $\cE_i=\cE(C^{(i)};\epsilon)$ and lengths $L_i$,
$\sum_i L_i=T$. Split each epoch into its informed and blind rounds,
$\Ep_i=S_i\sqcup\bar S_i$, and put $\ell_i=|S_i|$.

\emph{Step 1 (within-epoch regret on the informed subsequence).}
\Cref{alg:pgc} feeds the Polynomial-Weights sub-routine exactly the loss
vectors of the rounds in $S_i$, in order, over the fixed expert set
$\cE_i$, with losses in $[-1,1]$. \Cref{prop:hedge} with $\kappa=1$ and
horizon $\ell_i$ therefore gives
\begin{multline}
\max_{\pvec\in\cE_i}\sum_{t\in S_i}U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)\\
-\EE\Bigl[\sum_{t\in S_i}U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec_t),\pvec_t)\Bigr]
\;\le\;c_1\sqrt{\ell_i\log|\cE_i|}.
\label{eq:pr-hedge}
\end{multline}
Freezing the weights on $\bar S_i$ is invisible to this bound: the
sub-routine simply never sees those rounds.

\emph{Step 2 (passage to $\pvec^{*}$).} By construction every $t\in S_i$
has $a_t\in C^{(i)}$, which is precisely the hypothesis of
\Cref{lem:43}. Applying it to the catalogue $C^{(i)}$ and the
sub-sequence $(a_t)_{t\in S_i}$ of length $\ell_i$, and then using
$\pvec^{*}\in\cP$ as a feasible competitor exactly as in Step~4b of
\Cref{sec:proof},
\begin{equation}
\max_{\pvec\in\cE_i}\sum_{t\in S_i}U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec),\pvec)
\;\ge\;\sum_{t\in S_i}U_d(b_{a_t}(\pvec^{*}),\pvec^{*})-2\epsilon\ell_i .
\label{eq:pr-lem43}
\end{equation}

\emph{Step 3 (assembling).} Chaining \Cref{eq:pr-hedge,eq:pr-lem43},
summing over $i=0,\dots,M'$, adding \Cref{lem:blind} for the blind rounds
and using $\sum_i\ell_i\le T$,
\begin{multline*}
\EE[\Regret(T)]\;\le\;2\epsilon T
+c_1\sqrt{\log\Nmax}\sum_{i=0}^{M'}\sqrt{\ell_i}\\
+2\min\{MP,T\},
\end{multline*}
where $\log|\cE_i|\le\log\Nmax$ uniformly by \Cref{lem:logN-fixed}. By
Cauchy--Schwarz, $\sum_{i=0}^{M'}\sqrt{\ell_i}\le\sqrt{(M'+1)T}\le\sqrt{(M+1)T}$,
and choosing $\epsilon=1/\sqrt T$ gives \Cref{eq:pr-exact}.
\Cref{eq:pr} follows from \Cref{eq:Mbound} and \Cref{lem:logN-fixed}.
\end{proof}

Setting $P=1$ in \Cref{eq:pr} gives $M\le\Kmax$ and $MP\le\Kmax$, i.e.\
$O(\sqrt{T\Kmax\Lam}+\Kmax)$, which is \Cref{thm:main}: the analysis
degenerates to the original one, as it must.

\begin{remark}[Why the restart must be lazy]
\label{rem:naive}
The first analysis one is tempted to write treats every block as an epoch,
because the block boundaries are exactly the points at which the defender
\emph{could} learn something. That gives $B=\lceil T/P\rceil$ epochs of
length $P$ and, through Step~9,
$\sum_{j=1}^{B}\sqrt{P}=T/\sqrt P$, hence a bound of the shape
\[
O\Bigl(\sqrt{T\cdot(T/P)\cdot\Lam}\Bigr).
\]
This is correct but wasteful. Restarting Polynomial Weights on a block
whose catalogue is unchanged throws away a converged weight vector for
nothing, and the count $T/P$ ignores the fact that the catalogue can grow
at most $\Kmax$ times in total. The lazy restart of \Cref{alg:pgc}
replaces $T/P$ by $\min\{\Kmax,T/P\}$, which is never worse and is better
by a factor $\sqrt{T/(P\Kmax)}$ whenever $P<T/\Kmax$. The genuinely new
term in \Cref{eq:pr} is not the epoch count at all --- it is the
blind-round term $MP$.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Optimal period]
\label{cor:optP}
Assume the regime of \Cref{cor:regime}, i.e.\ $\Kmax\Lam\le T$, and set
\[
\Pstar\;\coloneqq\;\sqrt{\frac{T\,\Lam}{\Kmax}}
\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{T\,(n^2+\Kmax\log n)}{\Kmax}} .
\]
Then $\Pstar\le T/\Kmax$, and:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.05em,topsep=0.15em]
  \item \textup{(Batching is free.)} For every $P\le\Pstar$,
  \[
  \EE[\Regret(T)]=O\bigl(\sqrt{T\,\Kmax\,\Lam}\bigr),
  \]
  the rate of \Cref{thm:main}. Periodic revelation with any period up to
  $\Pstar$ costs nothing asymptotically.
  \item \textup{(No-regret threshold.)} \Cref{alg:pgc} is a no-regret
  algorithm whenever $P=o(T/\Kmax)$, and \Cref{eq:pr} becomes vacuous
  (linear in $T$) once $P=\Omega(T/\Kmax)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$\Pstar\le T/\Kmax$ is equivalent to $T\Lam/\Kmax\le T^2/\Kmax^2$, i.e.\
to $\Kmax\Lam\le T$, which is the standing assumption. For (i), $P\le\Pstar\le T/\Kmax$
gives $M\le\Kmax$ and
$2MP\le 2\Kmax\Pstar=2\sqrt{T\Kmax\Lam}$, so the blind-round term in
\Cref{eq:pr} is dominated by the learning term. For (ii), when
$P\le T/\Kmax$ we have $M\le\Kmax$ and the bound is
$O(\sqrt{T\Kmax\Lam}+\Kmax P)$, which is $o(T)$ iff $\Kmax P=o(T)$, using
$\Kmax\Lam=o(T)$ for the first term; when $P\ge T/\Kmax$ we have
$M=\lceil T/P\rceil$ and $MP\ge T$, so \Cref{eq:pr} exceeds $T$.
\end{proof}

Two readings of \Cref{cor:optP} are worth separating. If $P$ is a fact
about the environment, the corollary says how much staleness the defender
can tolerate before the analysis stops being informative:
intelligence delivered at least once every
$\Theta(n\sqrt{T/\Kmax})$ rounds (the value of $\Pstar$ when
$\Kmax\lesssim n^2/\log n$) is as good as instantaneous. If instead $P$ is
a design parameter --- how often to convene the analysts --- the corollary
says there is no reason to convene more often than every $\Pstar$ rounds,
because the regret bound is flat below that.

\subsubsection{A lower bound: batching strengthens the adversary}

\Cref{cor:optP}(ii) is an upper-bound statement, and one should ask whether
the degeneration at $P=\Omega(T/\Kmax)$ is an artefact. It is not.
\Cref{thm:pr-lower} shows that batching transplants the hardness of
\citet[Theorem 7.1]{balcan2015} from round granularity to block
granularity, and in doing so it slashes the number of attacker types the
adversary needs in order to force linear regret.

\begin{theorem}[Lower bound under periodic revelation]
\label{thm:pr-lower}
Fix $P\ge 1$ and $T\ge 4P$, and let $B=\lfloor T/P\rfloor$. There is a
$\mathrm{PR}$-$P$ repeated security game with $n=3$ targets, one resource,
and a set $K$ of at most $2\,(8P)^{B}$ attacker types on which every
online algorithm --- deterministic or randomised --- has expected regret at
least $T/2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We use the game of \citet[Theorem 7.1]{balcan2015}: $N=\{1,2,3\}$, one
resource, $U_d(1,\pvec)=U_d(3,\pvec)=-1$ and $U_d(2,\pvec)=0$. Because the
defender strictly prefers target $2$ to be attacked, setting $p_2=0$ never
hurts, so we may restrict attention to $\pvec=[p,0,1-p]$ with $p\in[0,1]$
and identify a strategy with the scalar $p$. By
\citet[Lemma 7.2]{balcan2015}, for any $0\le r_1\le r_2\le1$ there is an
attacker type $\alpha$ whose best response is target $1$ on $[0,r_1]$,
target $2$ on $(r_1,r_2]$ and target $3$ on $(r_2,1]$; the defender's
payoff against $\alpha$ is therefore $0$ if $p\in(r_1,r_2]$ and $-1$
otherwise.

Let $c=\lceil\log_2(4P)\rceil$, so that $4P\le 2^{c}\le 8P$. Build a
$2^{c}$-ary tree of depth $B$ whose level-$j$ nodes are the $2^{jc}$ dyadic
sub-intervals of $(0,1]$ of length $2^{-jc}$, each node's children
partitioning it. Associate with a node $v$, carrying interval
$I_v=(l_v,r_v]$, the type $\alpha_v$ obtained from
\citeauthor{balcan2015}'s Lemma 7.2 with $r_1=l_v$, $r_2=r_v$. The total
number of types is $\sum_{j=1}^{B}2^{jc}\le 2\cdot 2^{Bc}\le 2\,(8P)^{B}$.

\emph{The adversary.} Draw a uniformly random root-to-leaf path
$v_1,\dots,v_B$, each $v_j$ uniform among the $2^{c}$ children of
$v_{j-1}$, and play $\alpha_{v_j}$ at every round of block $\cB_j$ (and
$\alpha_{v_B}$ in any leftover rounds). The identifiers of the level-$j$
types are permuted uniformly at random, so an identifier carries no
information about which child was chosen.

\emph{The benchmark.} The intervals are nested,
$I_{v_B}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq I_{v_1}$. Any $\pvec^{*}$ with
$p^{*}\in I_{v_B}$ therefore satisfies $b_{\alpha_{v_j}}(\pvec^{*})=2$ for
every $j$, so $\pvec^{*}$ collects utility $0$ in every one of the $T$
rounds.

\emph{The defender's information inside block $j$.} At the start of block
$j$ the checkpoints have disclosed the dossiers of
$\alpha_{v_1},\dots,\alpha_{v_{j-1}}$, hence $I_{v_{j-1}}$, but nothing
about $\alpha_{v_j}$. During the block the only information the defender
receives is its own payoff, which equals $0$ if $p_t\in I_{v_j}$ and $-1$
otherwise; it does not distinguish target $1$ from target $3$. So each
round reveals a single bit: whether the strategy played landed in the
unknown child interval.

\emph{Failure probability.} Condition on the history $H_r$ after $r$
rounds of block $j$ in which every bit was $0$. Each round's $p_t$ lies in
at most one child of $I_{v_{j-1}}$, so at most $r$ children have been
excluded, and the posterior law of $v_j$ given $H_r$ is uniform on the
remaining $2^{c}-r$ children --- and independent of the algorithm's
internal randomness at round $r+1$. Hence
$\Pr[\text{round }r+1\text{ succeeds}\mid H_r]\le 1/(2^{c}-r)$, and
\begin{align*}
\Pr\bigl[\exists\,t\in\cB_j:\;p_t\in I_{v_j}\bigr]
&\;\le\;\sum_{r=0}^{P-1}\frac{1}{2^{c}-r}\\
&\;\le\;\frac{P}{2^{c}-P}\;\le\;\frac{P}{3P}\;=\;\frac13 .
\end{align*}
On the complementary event every one of the $|\cB_j|$ rounds of the block
pays $-1$, so the expected penalty in block $j$ is at least
$\tfrac23|\cB_j|$.

\emph{Conclusion.} Summing over the $B$ blocks, the expected total penalty
is at least $\tfrac23\,BP\ge\tfrac23(T-P)\ge\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac34 T=\tfrac T2$,
using $T\ge 4P$. Since $\pvec^{*}$ has utility $0$, the expected regret is
at least $T/2$, the expectation being over the adversary's randomisation
and the algorithm's. By Yao's minimax principle there is a deterministic
choice of path achieving at least this value against the given randomised
algorithm.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[How much batching costs the defender]
\label{rem:budget}
\Cref{thm:pr-lower} is best read as a statement about the adversary's
budget. To force regret $T/2$ against an immediately-informed defender,
\citet[Theorem 7.1]{balcan2015} need $2^{T+1}$ attacker types. Under
$\mathrm{PR}$-$P$ the same regret is forced with
$2(8P)^{T/P}=2^{\Theta((T/P)\log P)}$ types --- for $P=\sqrt T$, that is
$2^{\Theta(\sqrt T\log T)}$ instead of $2^{\Theta(T)}$. Batching does not
merely delay information; it makes a far smaller type set sufficient to
defeat any learner. Setting $P=1$ recovers a (slightly lossier) form of
\citeauthor{balcan2015}'s bound, as it should.

We do \emph{not} claim that the blind-round term $MP$ of \Cref{eq:pr} is
tight for small $\Kmax$: \Cref{thm:pr-lower} needs a type budget
exponential in $B$, whereas the upper bound is stated for $|K|\le\Kmax$.
Closing that gap --- in particular, deciding whether $\Theta(\Kmax P)$ is
the right blind-round cost when $\Kmax$ is a constant --- is left open; see
\Cref{sec:ext-open}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Direction 2: side information with an unknown type set}
\label{sec:contextual}

\citet{harris2024side} add \emph{side information} to the Stackelberg
model: before the leader commits, both players observe a context
$z_t\in\cZ$, and both players' payoffs may depend on it. Their benchmark
is the best \emph{policy} in hindsight, a map from contexts to strategies.
They show that no-regret learning is impossible when contexts and follower
types are both adversarial, and recover no-regret under either of two
relaxations. Their entire development assumes, as
\citet{balcan2015} do, that the set of $K$ follower types is known in
advance. This subsection asks what happens when it is not.

\subsubsection{Model}

\begin{problem}[Contextual unknown-$K$ repeated SSG]
\label{prob:ctx}
A context space $\cZ\subseteq\RR^d$ is fixed. At each round
$t\in\{1,\dots,T\}$:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.05em,topsep=0.15em]
  \item Nature reveals $z_t\in\cZ$ to both players;
  \item the defender commits to $\pvec_t\in\cP$, possibly drawn from a
  \emph{policy} $\pi_t:\cZ\to\cP$;
  \item attacker $a_t$ observes $(z_t,\pvec_t)$ and attacks
  $b_{a_t}(z_t,\pvec_t)=\argmax_{i\in N}U_{a_t}(i,\pvec_t;z_t)$;
  \item the defender receives $U_d(b_{a_t}(z_t,\pvec_t),\pvec_t;z_t)$ and
  observes $a_t$; if $a_t$ has not been seen before, its utility functions
  are revealed.
\end{enumerate}
Payoffs keep the form of \Cref{eq:Ud} at each context:
$U_d(i,\pvec;z)=u^c_d(z,i)\,p_i+u^u_d(z,i)(1-p_i)$ and
$U_\alpha(i,\pvec;z)=u^c_\alpha(z,i)\,p_i+u^u_\alpha(z,i)(1-p_i)$,
so both are linear in $\pvec$ for each fixed $(i,z)$. Following
\citet{harris2024side} we normalise $U_d\in[0,1]$ rather than $[-1,1]$;
this only rescales constants. The type set $K$ is unknown with
$|K|\le\Kmax$. The benchmark is the best policy in hindsight,
\[
\pi^{*}(z)\in\argmax_{\pvec\in\cP}\;\sum_{t=1}^{T}
U_d\bigl(b_{a_t}(z,\pvec),\pvec;z\bigr)\,\mathbf 1\{z_t=z\},
\]
and the \emph{contextual Stackelberg regret} is
$R(T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}U_d(b_{a_t}(z_t,\pi^{*}(z_t)),\pi^{*}(z_t);z_t)
-U_d(b_{a_t}(z_t,\pvec_t),\pvec_t;z_t)$.
\end{problem}

\subsubsection{Catalogue-indexed contextual best-response regions}

The structural prerequisite for everything below is a catalogue-relative
version of \citeauthor{harris2024side}'s Definitions 4.1--4.3, i.e.\ the
contextual analogue of \Cref{eq:Pia,eq:Psigma}.

\begin{definition}[Contextual best-response regions over a catalogue]
\label{def:ctx-regions}
Fix a context $z\in\cZ$ and a catalogue $C\subseteq K$. For a type
$\alpha\in C$ and a target $i\in N$ put
$\cP^{\alpha}_{i}(z)=\{\pvec\in\cP: b_\alpha(z,\pvec)=i\}$, the
intersection of $\cP$ with the $n-1$ linear inequalities
$U_\alpha(i,\pvec;z)\ge U_\alpha(j,\pvec;z)$, $j\ne i$. For a response
profile $\sigma:C\to N$ put
$\cP_\sigma(z;C)=\bigcap_{\alpha\in C}\cP^{\alpha}_{\sigma(\alpha)}(z)$,
and let $\Sigma_z(C)$ be the set of profiles with non-empty region. Each
$\cP_\sigma(z;C)$ is a bounded convex polytope, not necessarily closed,
and $|\Sigma_z(C)|\le n^{|C|}$. Finally, $\cE_z(C;\delta)$ denotes the set
of $\delta$-approximate extreme points of the family
$\{\cP_\sigma(z;C)\}_{\sigma\in\Sigma_z(C)}$, built exactly as in
\Cref{lem:43}: it contains every extreme point of $\mathrm{cl}(\cP_\sigma(z;C))$
that lies in $\cP_\sigma(z;C)$, and otherwise a point of
$\cP_\sigma(z;C)$ within $\ell_1$-distance $\delta$ of it.
\end{definition}

Two properties of \Cref{def:ctx-regions} are used repeatedly.

\begin{lemma}[Refinement and a uniform size bound]
\label{lem:refine}
Fix $z\in\cZ$ and catalogues $C\subseteq C'\subseteq K$ with
$|C'|\le\Kmax$. Then
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.05em,topsep=0.15em]
  \item \textup{(Refinement.)} Every cell $\cP_{\sigma'}(z;C')$ is
  contained in the cell $\cP_{\sigma'|_C}(z;C)$; hence the $C'$-partition
  of $\cP$ refines the $C$-partition, and every extreme point of a
  $C$-cell is a vertex of the hyperplane arrangement defining the
  $C'$-partition.
  \item \textup{(Uniform size.)} For every $\delta>0$,
  $\log|\cE_z(C;\delta)|\le\log\Nmax=O(n^2+\Kmax\log n)$, uniformly in $z$
  and in $C$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(i) If $\pvec\in\cP_{\sigma'}(z;C')$ then $b_\alpha(z,\pvec)=\sigma'(\alpha)$
for every $\alpha\in C'\supseteq C$, so $\pvec\in\cP_{\sigma'|_C}(z;C)$.
The half-spaces defining the $C$-partition form a subset of those defining
the $C'$-partition, so any point that is the intersection of $n$ linearly
independent members of the former is also such an intersection of members
of the latter.

(ii) At a fixed $z$ the counting argument of \Cref{lem:44} applies
verbatim: the regions are cut out by the $O(2^n+n)$ half-spaces defining
$\cP$ together with $\binom n2$ indifference hyperplanes per type in $C$,
so there are $O(2^n+n+|C|n^2)$ defining half-spaces and at most
$O((2^n+|C|n^2)^n)$ extreme points, each spawning at most $n^{|C|}$
$\delta$-approximate points. With $|C|\le\Kmax$ this is at most $\Nmax$,
and \Cref{lem:logN-fixed} gives the stated logarithm. The bound does not
involve $z$.
\end{proof}

Part (ii) is what makes the epoch machinery of \Cref{sec:proof} usable in
the contextual setting: although the defender must recompute
$\cE_{z_t}(C_t;\delta)$ at every round (the context space is not assumed
finite, so these sets cannot be precomputed), their \emph{cardinality} is
controlled uniformly by the same $\Nmax$.

\subsubsection{The fully adversarial setting remains unlearnable, even for \texorpdfstring{$\Kmax=2$}{Kmax=2}}

In the non-contextual model, \Cref{rem:necessity} is the whole reason
\Cref{prob:unknownK} is interesting: \citet[Theorem 7.1]{balcan2015} rule
out no-regret learning when $|K|$ is unrestricted, and \Cref{thm:main}
shows that bounding $|K|$ by a known $\Kmax$ restores it. The first thing
to check in the contextual model is whether the same repair works. It does
not, and it fails as badly as possible.

\begin{theorem}[Impossibility with two attacker types]
\label{thm:ctx-lower}
There is a contextual repeated security game in the sense of
\Cref{prob:ctx}, with $n=2$ targets, one resource, context space
$\cZ=[0,1]$ and exactly two attacker types, on which every online
algorithm suffers expected contextual Stackelberg regret $\Omega(T)$ when
the sequences $z_1,\dots,z_T$ and $a_1,\dots,a_T$ are chosen
adversarially. The bound holds even if the defender is told $K$, both
dossiers and $\Kmax=2$ before the game starts.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{The instance.} Take $N=\{1,2\}$, one resource with schedules
$\{\{1\},\{2\}\}$, so $\cP=\{\pvec\in[0,1]^2:p_1+p_2\le 1\}$. Let the
defender's payoffs be context-independent with $u^c_d(i)=1$ and
$u^u_d(i)=0$, so that
\[
U_d(i,\pvec)=p_i\in[0,1] :
\]
the defender scores $1$ if the attacked target happens to be covered and
$0$ otherwise. Since $U_d$ is non-decreasing in $p_i$ and, as we now
arrange, the attackers' best responses do not depend on $\pvec$, we may
assume $p_1+p_2=1$ without loss of generality and write $g=p_1$.

Let $\alpha_1$ have $u^c_{\alpha_1}(z,1)=u^u_{\alpha_1}(z,1)=1$ and
$u^c_{\alpha_1}(z,2)=u^u_{\alpha_1}(z,2)=0$ for every $z$; then
$U_{\alpha_1}(1,\pvec;z)=1>0=U_{\alpha_1}(2,\pvec;z)$, so
$b_{\alpha_1}(z,\pvec)=1$ for all $z,\pvec$. Symmetrically let $\alpha_2$
always attack target $2$. Thus $K=\{\alpha_1,\alpha_2\}$ and $\Kmax=2$.
The defender's realised payoff is
\begin{equation}
U_d\bigl(b_{a_t}(z_t,\pvec_t),\pvec_t\bigr)
= g_t\,\mathbf 1\{a_t=\alpha_1\}+(1-g_t)\,\mathbf 1\{a_t=\alpha_2\}.
\label{eq:ctx-payoff}
\end{equation}

\emph{The reduction.} Recall the online linear thresholding problem
\citep[Lemma 3.1]{harris2024side}: an adversary fixes an unknown cutoff
$s\in[0,1]$; each round it reveals $\omega_t\in[0,1]$, the learner
outputs $g_t\in[0,1]$, the label is $y_t=+1$ if $\omega_t>s$ and $-1$
otherwise, and the learner's utility is
$u^{\mathrm{OLT}}(\omega_t,g_t)=g_t\mathbf 1\{y_t=1\}+(1-g_t)\mathbf 1\{y_t=-1\}$.
No algorithm achieves $o(T)$ regret against the optimal thresholding
policy, which scores $1$ every round \citep[Lemma 3.1]{harris2024side}.

Given any algorithm $\mathrm{ALG}$ for \Cref{prob:ctx}, run it on the
instance above with $z_t=\omega_t$, take $g_t=p_{t,1}$ as the thresholding
guess, and set
$a_t=\alpha_1$ if $y_t=+1$ and $a_t=\alpha_2$ if $y_t=-1$. By
\Cref{eq:ctx-payoff} the per-round payoff of $\mathrm{ALG}$ equals
$u^{\mathrm{OLT}}(\omega_t,g_t)$.

\emph{The benchmark.} The label $y_t$ is a deterministic function of
$z_t=\omega_t$, so all rounds sharing a context share an attacker type;
hence the optimal policy is $\pi^{*}(z)=(1,0)$ for $z>s$ and
$\pi^{*}(z)=(0,1)$ for $z\le s$, which scores $U_d=1$ in every round.
Therefore
\[
R(T)\;=\;T-\sum_{t=1}^{T}u^{\mathrm{OLT}}(\omega_t,g_t)\;=\;R^{\mathrm{OLT}}(T).
\]
If $\mathrm{ALG}$ had $o(T)$ contextual Stackelberg regret we would obtain
an $o(T)$-regret online linear thresholding algorithm, contradicting
\citet[Lemma 3.1]{harris2024side}. Nothing in the argument uses ignorance
of $K$, so the conclusion holds even for a defender given $K$ in advance.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The cardinality bound is powerless with side information]
\label{cor:ctx-kmax-useless}
For every $\Kmax\ge 2$ there is a contextual repeated SSG with
$|K|\le\Kmax$ on which no algorithm is no-regret. In particular, the
device that makes \Cref{prob:unknownK} tractable --- replacing ``$K$
unknown'' by ``$K$ unknown but $|K|\le\Kmax$'' --- does not make
\Cref{prob:ctx} tractable.
\end{corollary}

It is worth being explicit about where the hardness sits, because it is
not where one might expect. The instance of \Cref{thm:ctx-lower} has
\emph{context-independent} payoffs for both players: the game played at
every round is literally the same $2\times2$ game. What the context does
is index the benchmark. Competing with the best \emph{policy}
$\pi^{*}:\cZ\to\cP$ rather than with the best fixed strategy is what makes
the problem hard, because the adversary can encode the identity of $a_t$
inside $z_t$ through a relationship that is itself not learnable online.
Bounding $|K|$ limits how many distinct attacker behaviours exist; it says
nothing about how they are correlated with the context, and the
correlation is the difficulty. Following \citet{harris2024side}, we
therefore restrict the adversary; \Cref{tab:ctx-summary} summarises the
resulting picture, which \Cref{sec:ext-open} discusses.

\subsubsection{Stochastic contexts, adversarial types}
\label{sec:ctx-stochz}

Here $z_1,\dots,z_T$ are drawn i.i.d.\ from an unknown distribution
$\cD_z$ on $\cZ$, while $a_1,\dots,a_T\in K^T$ is chosen in advance by an
oblivious adversary that knows $\cD_z$ (the same adversary model as
\Cref{thm:main}). Performance is measured by
$\EE[R(T)]$, the expectation being over the draw of the contexts and the
algorithm's randomisation, against the policy $\pi^{*}$ that is optimal
given $\cD_z$ and $a_1,\dots,a_T$
\citep[Definition 4.9]{harris2024side}.

\citet[Theorem 4.11]{harris2024side} solve the known-$K$ case by running
Hedge over a finite policy class indexed by a grid of weight vectors on
the simplex $\Delta_K$. When $K$ is unknown, that class is not available
at the start; the natural fix is the one this paper already uses --- grow
it, and restart.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Contextual-Grow-Catalogue} (stochastic contexts).}
\label{alg:cgc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\small
\Require horizon $T$, game primitives, $\delta\gets 1/T$
\State $C\gets\emptyset$
\For{$t=1,\dots,T$}
  \State observe $z_t$ and compute $\cE_{z_t}(C;\delta)$
  \State sample $\pi_t\sim p_t$ from the current Hedge distribution over $\Pi(C)$; play $\pvec_t=\pi_t(z_t)$
  \State observe $a_t$; if $a_t\notin C$, record its dossier
  \State for each $\pi^{(\omega)}\in\Pi(C)$ set
         $\ell_t[\pi^{(\omega)}]\gets -U_d\bigl(b_{a_t}(z_t,\pi^{(\omega)}(z_t)),\pi^{(\omega)}(z_t);z_t\bigr)$
         and update Hedge
  \If{$a_t\notin C$} \Comment{catalogue growth: rebuild and restart}
    \State $C\gets C\cup\{a_t\}$;~$\Pi(C)\gets\{\pi^{(\omega)}\}_{\omega\in\Omega(C)}$;~reset Hedge on $\Pi(C)$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Concretely, for a catalogue $C$ let
\begin{align*}
\Omega(C)&=\bigl\{\omega\in\Delta_{|C|}:T\,\omega[\alpha]\in\NN\ \forall\alpha\in C\bigr\},\\
\pi^{(\omega)}(z)&=\argmax_{\pvec\in\cE_z(C;\delta)}\sum_{\alpha\in C}
\omega[\alpha]\,U_d\bigl(b_\alpha(z,\pvec),\pvec;z\bigr),
\end{align*}
and $\Pi(C)=\{\pi^{(\omega)}:\omega\in\Omega(C)\}$, so that
\begin{equation}
|\Pi(C)|\;\le\;|\Omega(C)|=\binom{T+|C|-1}{|C|-1}\;\le\;T^{|C|}\;\le\;T^{\Kmax}.
\label{eq:Pisize}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Stochastic contexts, adversarial types, unknown $K$]
\label{thm:ctx-stochz}
Let the defender play \Cref{alg:cgc} in the setting of \Cref{prob:ctx}
with i.i.d.\ contexts and an oblivious adversarial type sequence with
$|K|\le\Kmax$. Then
\[
\EE[R(T)]\;=\;O\Bigl(\Kmax\sqrt{T\log T}\;+\;\Kmax^{2}\Bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Because the adversary is oblivious, the discovery rounds
$\tau_1<\dots<\tau_{K'}$, the epochs $\Ep_1,\dots,\Ep_{K'}$ of
\Cref{eq:epoch-def}, their catalogues $C_i$ and their lengths $L_i$ are all
deterministic, and $K'\le\Kmax$. Fix an epoch $i$; by construction
$a_t\in C_i$ for every $t\in\Ep_i$.

\emph{(a) From $\pi^{*}$ to a policy into $\cE_\cdot(C_i;\delta)$.}
\citet[Lemma 4.4]{harris2024side}, applied at each context with the
catalogue $C_i$ and the sub-sequence $(a_t)_{t\in\Ep_i}$, produces a policy
$\pi^{(E)}_i$ with $\pi^{(E)}_i(z)\in\cE_z(C_i;\delta)$ and
\begin{multline*}
\sum_{t\in\Ep_i}\!\Bigl(U_d\bigl(b_{a_t}(z_t,\pi^{*}(z_t)),\pi^{*}(z_t);z_t\bigr)\\
-U_d\bigl(b_{a_t}(z_t,\pi^{(E)}_i(z_t)),\pi^{(E)}_i(z_t);z_t\bigr)\Bigr)
\;\le\;\delta L_i .
\end{multline*}
The hypothesis of that lemma is exactly that only types of $C_i$ occur in
the sub-sequence, which is \Cref{eq:epoch-types}. Summing over epochs, the
total cost is $\delta\sum_i L_i\le\delta T=1$.

\emph{(b) From $\pi^{(E)}_i$ to the grid $\Pi(C_i)$.} Since the contexts
are i.i.d.\ and the type sequence is fixed, for any policy $\pi$
\begin{multline}
\EE_{z}\Bigl[\sum_{t\in\Ep_i}U_d\bigl(b_{a_t}(z_t,\pi(z_t)),\pi(z_t);z_t\bigr)\Bigr]\\
=\sum_{\alpha\in C_i}\nu_\alpha\,
\EE_{z\sim\cD_z}\Bigl[U_d\bigl(b_\alpha(z,\pi(z)),\pi(z);z\bigr)\Bigr],
\label{eq:ctx-lin}
\end{multline}
where $\nu_\alpha=|\{t\in\Ep_i:a_t=\alpha\}|$ and $\sum_\alpha\nu_\alpha=L_i$;
this is \citet[Lemma 4.10]{harris2024side} specialised to a fixed type
sequence. So the epoch value of a policy depends on it only through the
$|C_i|$ numbers $v_\alpha(\pi)=\EE_z[U_d(b_\alpha(z,\pi(z)),\pi(z);z)]\in[0,1]$,
weighted by $\nu$. Let $\omega^{*}\in\Omega(C_i)$ round the probability
vector $\nu/L_i$ onto the grid by setting
$T\omega^{*}[\alpha]=\lfloor T\nu_\alpha/L_i\rfloor$ and distributing the
at most $|C_i|$ leftover units arbitrarily, so that
$\sum_{\alpha}|\omega^{*}[\alpha]-\nu_\alpha/L_i|\le |C_i|/T$. The
policy $\pi^{(\omega^{*})}$ maximises the $\omega^{*}$-weighted objective
over $\cE_z(C_i;\delta)$ pointwise in $z$, hence over the class containing
$\pi^{(E)}_i$; transferring from the $\omega^{*}$-weighting to the
$\nu$-weighting costs at most
$2L_i\cdot|C_i|/T\le 2|C_i|\le 2\Kmax$ because $v_\alpha(\cdot)\in[0,1]$.
Therefore
\[
\EE_z\Bigl[\sum_{t\in\Ep_i}U_d\bigl(\cdot,\pi^{(\omega^{*})}\bigr)\Bigr]
\;\ge\;\EE_z\Bigl[\sum_{t\in\Ep_i}U_d\bigl(\cdot,\pi^{(E)}_i\bigr)\Bigr]-2\Kmax .
\]

\emph{(c) Hedge inside the epoch.} \Cref{alg:cgc} runs Hedge over the
fixed class $\Pi(C_i)$ for $L_i$ rounds with losses in $[-1,0]$, so
\Cref{prop:hedge} and \Cref{eq:Pisize} give
\begin{multline*}
\max_{\pi\in\Pi(C_i)}\sum_{t\in\Ep_i}U_d(\cdot,\pi)
-\EE\Bigl[\sum_{t\in\Ep_i}U_d(\cdot,\pvec_t)\Bigr]\\
\;\le\;c_1\sqrt{L_i\log|\Pi(C_i)|}\;\le\;c_1\sqrt{L_i\,\Kmax\log T}.
\end{multline*}

\emph{(d) Assembling.} Adding (a)--(c) over the $K'\le\Kmax$ epochs, and
paying at most $2$ per discovery round exactly as in Step~6 of
\Cref{sec:proof},
\begin{multline*}
\EE[R(T)]\;\le\;1+2\Kmax+2\Kmax^{2}\\
+c_1\sqrt{\Kmax\log T}\sum_{i=1}^{K'}\sqrt{L_i}.
\end{multline*}
Cauchy--Schwarz gives $\sum_i\sqrt{L_i}\le\sqrt{K'T}\le\sqrt{\Kmax T}$, so
the last term is $O(\Kmax\sqrt{T\log T})$, and the claim follows.
\end{proof}

Compared with the known-$K$ rate $O(\sqrt{KT\log T}+K)$ of
\citet[Theorem 4.11]{harris2024side}, \Cref{thm:ctx-stochz} pays a
multiplicative $\sqrt{\Kmax}$ and an additive $\Kmax^2$. The multiplicative
factor arises in exactly the same place as in \Cref{thm:main} --- the
Cauchy--Schwarz step over the epochs forced by restarting Hedge --- so the
price of an unknown catalogue is the same in the contextual and
non-contextual worlds.

\begin{remark}[Adaptive type adversaries]
\label{rem:adaptive}
\Cref{thm:ctx-stochz} is stated for an oblivious type adversary, matching
\Cref{thm:main}. It extends to an adaptive adversary that chooses $a_t$
knowing $\cD_z$ and $z_1,\dots,z_{t-1}$ but not $z_t$, at the cost of one
extra observation: both $a_t$ and the epoch index of round $t$ are
measurable with respect to $\sigma(a_1,\dots,a_t)$, and the adversary sees
only $z_1,\dots,z_{t-1}$ when it picks $a_t$; hence $z_t$ is independent
of the pair $(\mathbf 1\{a_t=\alpha\},\mathbf 1\{t\in\Ep_i\})$ and
\Cref{eq:ctx-lin}
survives with $\nu_\alpha$ replaced by
$\sum_t\Pr[a_t=\alpha,\,t\in\Ep_i]$. The rounding argument in step (b) is
then applied to those expectations, and the bound is unchanged.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Stochastic types, adversarial contexts}
\label{sec:ctx-stochf}

Now reverse the relaxation: $a_1,\dots,a_T$ are drawn i.i.d.\ from an
unknown distribution $\cF$ supported on the unknown set $K$, while the
contexts are adversarial (possibly adaptively, knowing $\cF$ and
$a_1,\dots,a_{t-1}$ but not $a_t$). Write
\[
q(z,\pvec)[i]\;=\;\Pr_{a\sim\cF}\bigl[b_a(z,\pvec)=i\bigr],\qquad i\in N,
\]
for the induced distribution over attacked targets, and let
\[
\widehat q_t(z,\pvec)[i]\;=\;\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{\tau<t}
\mathbf 1\{b_{a_\tau}(z,\pvec)=i\}
\]
be its empirical estimate. The defender plays greedily,
\begin{equation}
\pvec_t\;\in\;\argmax_{\pvec\in\cE_{z_t}(C_t;\delta)}\;\widehat V_t(z_t,\pvec),
\label{eq:greedy}
\end{equation}
where
$\widehat V_t(z,\pvec)=\sum_{i\in N}U_d(i,\pvec;z)\,\widehat q_t(z,\pvec)[i]$
and $\delta=1/T$. Note that $\widehat q_t$ is computable: it only ever
evaluates $b_{a_\tau}$ for types already observed, whose dossiers the
defender holds.

The reason this regime behaves completely differently from
\Cref{sec:ctx-stochz} is the following elementary observation, which says
that the defender's \emph{estimated} objective is piecewise linear with
respect to precisely the partition the defender can compute.

\begin{lemma}[Greedy over the catalogue is $\delta$-optimal for the estimate]
\label{lem:greedy}
Fix $t$ and $z$, and let $C_t=\{a_1,\dots,a_{t-1}\}$. Then
$\widehat V_t(z,\cdot)$ is constant in its $\widehat q_t$ factor on every
cell $\cP_\sigma(z;C_t)$ and therefore linear in $\pvec$ on that cell.
Consequently
\[
\max_{\pvec\in\cE_z(C_t;\delta)}\widehat V_t(z,\pvec)
\;\ge\;\max_{\pvec\in\cP}\widehat V_t(z,\pvec)-\delta .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $\tau<t$ we have $a_\tau\in C_t$, so $b_{a_\tau}(z,\pvec)=\sigma(a_\tau)$
for every $\pvec\in\cP_\sigma(z;C_t)$; hence $\widehat q_t(z,\cdot)$ is
constant on the cell, and $\widehat V_t(z,\cdot)$ is a fixed convex
combination of the linear functions $U_d(i,\cdot;z)$ there. Let
$\pvec\in\cP$ be arbitrary and $\sigma$ its cell. Maximising the linear
function $\widehat V_t(z,\cdot)$ over the closed convex polytope
$\mathrm{cl}(\cP_\sigma(z;C_t))$ is attained at an extreme point
$\pvec'$, and $\cE_z(C_t;\delta)$ contains a point
$\pvec''\in\cP_\sigma(z;C_t)$ with $\|\pvec'-\pvec''\|_1\le\delta$. Since
$u^c_d,u^u_d\in[0,1]$ we have
$|U_d(i,\pvec';z)-U_d(i,\pvec'';z)|\le\|\pvec'-\pvec''\|_1\le\delta$ for
every $i$, so
$\widehat V_t(z,\pvec'')\ge\widehat V_t(z,\pvec')-\delta\ge\widehat V_t(z,\pvec)-\delta$.
\end{proof}

\Cref{lem:greedy} is the crux. It does \emph{not} compare the defender's
feasible set with the finer set $\cE_z(K;\delta)$ that a defender knowing
$K$ could use. It says something better: relative to the objective the
defender is actually optimising, the coarse set $\cE_z(C_t;\delta)$ is
already optimal up to $\delta$, because the estimator and the partition
are built from the same observed types and refine together. There is
therefore no need to restart anything when a new type arrives, and no
epoch decomposition is required.

\begin{theorem}[Stochastic types, adversarial contexts, unknown $K$]
\label{thm:ctx-stochf}
Let the defender play \Cref{eq:greedy} in the setting of \Cref{prob:ctx}
with i.i.d.\ attacker types and adversarial contexts, and $|K|\le\Kmax$.
Then
\begin{multline*}
\EE[R(T)]\;\le\;1
+2\sum_{t=1}^{T}\EE\Bigl[\TV\bigl(q_t^{*},\widehat q_t^{*}\bigr)
+\TV\bigl(q_t,\widehat q_t\bigr)\Bigr],
\end{multline*}
where $q_t^{*}=q(z_t,\pi^{*}(z_t))$, $\widehat q_t^{*}=\widehat q_t(z_t,\pi^{*}(z_t))$,
$q_t=q(z_t,\pvec_t)$ and $\widehat q_t=\widehat q_t(z_t,\pvec_t)$. Hence,
with the concentration accounting of
\citet[Corollary 4.8]{harris2024side},
\[
\EE[R(T)]\;=\;O\bigl(n\sqrt{T\log T}\bigr).
\]
The bound contains no dependence on $\Kmax$ whatsoever.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $t$ and abbreviate $z=z_t$. Write $\EE_t[\cdot]$ for expectation over
$a_t\sim\cF$ given the history and $\widehat\EE_t[\cdot]$ for the same
expression with $q$ replaced by $\widehat q_t$, so that
$\widehat\EE_t[U_d(b_{a_t}(z,\pvec),\pvec;z)]=\widehat V_t(z,\pvec)$. Since
$U_d\in[0,1]$, for any $\pvec$
\begin{equation}
\bigl|\EE_t[U_d(b_{a_t}(z,\pvec),\pvec;z)]-\widehat V_t(z,\pvec)\bigr|
\;\le\;2\,\TV\bigl(q(z,\pvec),\widehat q_t(z,\pvec)\bigr).
\label{eq:tvgap}
\end{equation}
By \Cref{lem:greedy} applied with the comparator $\pi^{*}(z)\in\cP$,
$\widehat V_t(z,\pvec_t)\ge\widehat V_t(z,\pi^{*}(z))-\delta$. Combining
this with \Cref{eq:tvgap} at $\pvec=\pi^{*}(z)$ and at $\pvec=\pvec_t$,
\begin{multline*}
\EE_t\bigl[U_d(b_{a_t}(z,\pi^{*}(z)),\pi^{*}(z);z)\bigr]
\le\EE_t\bigl[U_d(b_{a_t}(z,\pvec_t),\pvec_t;z)\bigr]\\
+\delta+2\TV\bigl(q(z,\pi^{*}(z)),\widehat q_t\bigr)
+2\TV\bigl(q(z,\pvec_t),\widehat q_t\bigr).
\end{multline*}
Summing over $t$, taking expectations and using $\delta T=1$ gives the
first display. For the second, \citet[Corollary 4.8]{harris2024side} show
by Hoeffding's inequality that
$\EE[\TV(q(z,\pvec),\widehat q_t(z,\pvec))]\le n\sqrt{\log(2T)/(t-1)}+1/(2T)$,
and $\sum_{t=2}^{T}(t-1)^{-1/2}=O(\sqrt T)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[On the uniformity of the concentration]
\label{rem:union}
The bound $\EE[\TV]\lesssim n\sqrt{\log T/t}$ quoted above is the one
stated by \citet[Corollary 4.8]{harris2024side}; their union bound is
taken over rounds and targets. A bound that is uniform over all pairs
$(z,\pvec)$ needs a union over the distinct values the estimator can take,
i.e.\ over the at most $n^{|C_t|}\le n^{\Kmax}$ best-response profiles of
\Cref{def:ctx-regions}, which replaces $\log T$ by $\log T+\Kmax\log n$
and yields the self-contained bound
$\EE[R(T)]=O\bigl(n\sqrt{T(\log T+\Kmax\log n)}\bigr)$. Either way the
point stands: the term is present verbatim in the known-$K$ analysis, so
not knowing $K$ adds nothing.
\end{remark}

If instead the defender estimates the type distribution directly ---
the analogue of \citet[Corollary 4.7]{harris2024side}, with
$\widehat p_{t}(\alpha)=\frac1{t-1}\sum_{\tau<t}\mathbf 1\{a_\tau=\alpha\}$
supported on $C_t$ --- then unknown $K$ does cost something, but only an
additive constant per type.

\begin{proposition}[Type-frequency estimator]
\label{prop:missing-mass}
With the estimator $\widehat p_t$ above, which assigns zero mass to
every type not yet observed,
\begin{multline*}
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\EE\bigl[\text{mass of }\cF\text{ outside }C_t\bigr]\\
=\sum_{\alpha\in K}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\cF(\alpha)\bigl(1-\cF(\alpha)\bigr)^{t-1}
\;\le\;|K|\;\le\;\Kmax ,
\end{multline*}
and consequently the greedy algorithm driven by $\widehat p_t$ has
$\EE[R(T)]=O\bigl(\Kmax\sqrt{T\log T}+\Kmax\bigr)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Type $\alpha$ is absent from $C_t$ exactly when it has not occurred in the
first $t-1$ rounds, which has probability $(1-\cF(\alpha))^{t-1}$; the
expected missing mass at round $t$ is
$\sum_\alpha\cF(\alpha)(1-\cF(\alpha))^{t-1}$. Summing the geometric
series over $t$ gives at most $1$ per type. The missing mass bounds the
extra total variation incurred by $\widehat p_t$ relative to the
known-support estimator, so the regret bound of
\citet[Corollary 4.7]{harris2024side} picks up this additive term.
\end{proof}

\Cref{prop:missing-mass} makes the mechanism explicit: a type that is rare
enough to stay undiscovered is, for exactly that reason, rare enough to be
harmless. This is why stochastic attackers forgive an unknown catalogue
and adversarial ones do not.

\subsection{Summary and open problems}
\label{sec:ext-open}

\Cref{tab:ctx-summary} collects the picture for \Cref{sec:contextual}. The
non-contextual row is included for reference; $\Lam=n^2+\Kmax\log n$ as in
\Cref{eq:Lambda}.

Three questions are left open.

\paragraph{Bandit feedback with an unknown catalogue.} Both
\citet[Sec.~6]{balcan2015} and \citet[Sec.~5]{harris2024side} pass from
full to bandit feedback by constructing a barycentric spanner
\citep{awerbuch2008} for the space of best-response indicator vectors,
which lives in $\RR^{K}$ and therefore has a basis of size $K$. With an
unknown catalogue that space \emph{grows}, so the spanner must be
recomputed at every discovery round, and one has to bound the loss from
discarding the estimates built on the old basis. Combined with the
change-point detection needed to notice a new type at all, we expect a
bound of the form $O(T^{2/3}\mathrm{poly}(n,\Kmax))$ plus a
detection-delay term.

\paragraph{Lower bounds.} \Cref{thm:ctx-stochz} and \Cref{thm:main} both
pay $\sqrt{\Kmax}$ relative to their known-$K$ counterparts, and we do not
know whether that factor is necessary. Likewise, \Cref{thm:pr-lower}
leaves open whether the blind-round cost $\Theta(\Kmax P)$ of
\Cref{thm:pr} is real when $\Kmax$ is small (\Cref{rem:budget}).

\paragraph{Combining the two directions.} \Cref{prob:pr} and
\Cref{prob:ctx} are orthogonal relaxations and can be imposed together:
periodic revelation with side information. \Cref{thm:ctx-stochf} suggests
this is benign in the stochastic-attacker regime --- the greedy estimator
never needed the catalogue to be current --- whereas \Cref{thm:ctx-stochz}
would inherit both the $\sqrt{\Kmax}$ restart factor and the blind-round
term $\Kmax P$.
